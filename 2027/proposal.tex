\noindent \textbf{Description of the Research Program}

\section{Context, research gap, and opportunity}

National decarbonization policies and international climate agreements are largely guided by energy system models (ESMs) and integrated assessment models (IAMs),\ci{Pauliuk2017} whose community has converged on a small set of internally consistent socioeconomic and mitigation pathways: the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) and Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs).\ci{oneillNewScenarioFramework2014} Similarly, circular-economy roadmaps rest on macro-scale material flow analyses (MFAs).\ci{aistRoadmapIronSteel2025,alonsoWorldMineralsOutlook,canadaplasticspactRoadmap2025Shared2021} These tools guide economy-wide policy, but are too coarse to inform the thousands of decisions by consumers, firms and sectors that implement transitions.

These micro- and meso-scale decisions are instead increasingly guided by attributional life-cycle assessment (ALCA), which estimates the share of environmental impacts linked to a product or function, as in carbon footprints and environmental product declarations.\ci{Hellweg2014,hellwegLifecycleAssessmentGuide2023} Most ALCAs are \emph{retrospective}, relying on past data, which limits their use for forward-looking decisions in a rapidly transitioning economy. Prospective ALCA (p-ALCA) databases such as Premise now incorporate macro-scenario data,\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} bringing temporal consistency to the assessment of long-lived products and investments.

Major inconsistencies persist, however, between micro-scale p-ALCAs and macro-scale transition models. An industry already supplied by clean electricity will see, in its p-ALCA, little benefit in further improving its energy efficiency; yet energy system models often deem such gains essential to free clean electricity capacity for other sectors. What appears negligible in a static footprint can thus be critical in the dynamic transition context. Material cycles show the same problem: a p-ALCA may recommend maximizing the recycled content of a product, while dynamic MFAs show that limited in-use stocks will constrain scrap supply for years,\ci{Pauliuk2015TakingStock} so that more recycled content in one product may simply divert scarce secondary material from another. Both contradictions arise because p-ALCAs remain \emph{attributional}: limited to the value chains under study, they cannot capture the market-mediated, system-wide consequences that shape the transition.\ci{Heijungs2001,Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons}
% These inconsistencies arise because all ALCAs are fundamentally unconstrained, linear models;\ci{Heijungs2001} they do not capture material or resource limits that are central to macro-scale transition strategies.

Capturing such bottlenecks and market-mediated mechanisms has long been the aim of consequential LCA (CLCA):\ci{Zamagni2012} whereas ALCAs describe how impacts are distributed within the value chains of a given economy, CLCAs quantify the \emph{consequences} of a decision: how impacts change when it perturbs the economy.\ci{Ekvall2004} Yet, though theorized in the early 2000s, CLCA remains a niche approach.\ci{bamberComparingSourcesAnalysis2020} It requires identifying which suppliers would respond to a change in demand for each product (marginal suppliers),\ci{Weidema2003} which is highly uncertain and context-dependent.\ci{Mathiesen2009} Guidelines provide rules for this identification, and consequential databases have attempted to apply them across the economy,\ci{Weidema2003,vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} but with heterogeneous assumptions and little internal consistency; extensions of these rules to transition scenarios are only recent.\ci{maesProspectiveConsequentialLife2023,provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} These rules rely on market trends and scarce empirical data, and detailed modelling is often restricted to a few processes in the foreground of the product system, inconsistently with the background database. Building CLCAs has thus proven costly, and their results, resting on simplifications whose errors are largely unknown, are often hard to interpret and viewed with skepticism. Finally, CLCAs are almost always defined against a single scenario, the \emph{status quo} over the lifetime of the decision,\ci{Steubing2016} an assumption ill-suited to investment and production decisions in a rapidly evolving economy.


We see a \textbf{strategic opportunity} to make prospective CLCA (p-CLCA) a systematic bridge between macro-scale models (ESMs, IAMs, MFAs) and micro- and meso-scale decisions, anchoring each targetted analysis as a perturbation of broader representative scenarios so that LCA guidance aligns with transition pathways.


\section{Core challenges and objectives}


We address two core challenges of CLCA through a common strategy. The first (\textbf{Pillar I}) is to represent how markets respond to marginal changes in demand. Sophisticated economic models would ideally capture this, but cannot be run for the thousands of markets in a CLCA, which are instead represented by simplifying heuristics. These heuristics are currently (a) not bound by a consistent narrative, (b) of widely varying sophistication, with uncharacterized errors, and (c) inferred from trends rather than from the mechanisms that drive capacity investment (e.g., the timing of demand relative to peak in electricity systems). Our objective (\textbf{SO-1}) is therefore a scenario-consistent framework for identifying marginal suppliers: the responses of all markets in an assessment are derived from the same base scenario, by perturbing a model or applying heuristics to its outputs, informed by these mechanisms, and the errors of each level of simplification are characterized.

The second challenge (\textbf{Pillar II}) is to represent how technological processes respond to design and operating decisions and to deployment context. Sophisticated engineering models would ideally capture this, but cannot be built for the thousands of processes in a CLCA, which are instead represented with little parametrization or as fixed black boxes; prospective databases make these evolve along macro-scenarios by rescaling inputs and emissions, aligning them with the scenario but not necessarily with process physics.\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} These simplifications (a) prevent the assessment of design and operating decisions and (b) obscure differences between technology variants by location, context and age; identifying the right marginal supplier (Pillar~I) is of little use if all suppliers share the same recipe. They also (c) make LCA work inefficient: detailed engineering models, where used, are rarely formatted for reuse or interconnection, limiting collaboration on common models and their adaptation to new contexts. Our objective (\textbf{SO-2}) is therefore twofold: an interoperability framework that holds process models of any sophistication and represents technologies as instances of \emph{process-model classes}, instantiated from the base scenario for their context and technology evolution and from the decision under study for their design and operation; and an efficient workflow to populate these classes with reduced engineering models, from established to emerging processes.

In both pillars, our \textbf{common strategy} is to (a) mirror each conceptual framework in open-source software, to support a change in community practice: an integration framework for market responses, as an open layer on existing tools (Pillar~I), and a modelling framework for process-model classes, as a new open digital infrastructure (Pillar~II); (b) resolve what macro-scenarios aggregate, broad regions and technology classes, by representing the markets and processes needed in LCA as instances of broader ones, all derived from the same base scenario; (c) accommodate simplifying assumptions consistently, to fit differing data availability and resolution; and (d) test these simplifications against more sophisticated models or observations to characterize their uncertainties and biases. We pursue these through case studies with differing data availability for market and process causation, each evaluated under at least two contrasting SSP/RCP base scenarios, including a low-demand pathway.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025} Across our cases, we ask a \textbf{program-level question}: under which conditions do these representation choices change the decisions supported by p-CLCA?

These specific objectives contribute to our \textbf{long-term goal}: a p-CLCA practice built on shared, open representations of markets and technologies, maintained and extended by the community, that provides pragmatic, discriminating decision support for consumption, process design and operation in a world in transition. % Our strategic positionning is that this is achievable if we maximize the use (and reuse) of available data and models through a smart integration and a flexible, open modelling framework; consolidation of these models as disaggregations and perturbations of macro-scenarios; and a systematic, quantitative approach to simplifying assumptions in the representation of economic and engineering causal mechanisms.

%\textbf{Long-term goal.} Our long-term goal is a consequential LCA that can tell decision-makers how a specific choice of technology, design, operation or deployment changes environmental flows in a world in transition, and how far that answer can be trusted. This requires representations of markets and technologies that are general enough to cover the economy, specific enough to respond to the decisions and contexts that matter, consistent with transition scenarios, and tested so that their errors are known. Beyond this grant, we aim to contribute to an open, community-maintained ecosystem of such representations. We do not compete with economists or process engineers on their own models: we build reductions of these models suited to consequential LCA, connect them consistently, and measure what each simplification costs in terms of decisions.

\underline{Recent progress:} For market responses, we developed guidelines and a timing-shift method for deriving marginal suppliers from IAM scenarios,\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} calibrated the open-source ESM EnergyScope-Quebec, and integrated LCA metrics in ESM optimization.\ci{souttreMescalToolCoupling2025} For databases, we regionalized ecoinvent with trade data\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} and pushed forward its reconciliation with mass conservation.\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} For material flows, we built SSP-consistent aluminium scenarios,\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} quantified metal bottlenecks along IAM energy-transition scenarios,\ci{bieuvilleMetalBottlenecksEnergy2025} and assembled site-level databases on Canadian metal production.\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025,pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} For processes, we developed highly parametrized models of composting and hydrothermal carbonization.\ci{ravoahangyParametrizationBiowasteComposting2026,tanguay-riouxCanHydrothermalCarbonization2026} A prototype infrastructure for process models (v0) is being implemented with software-development support from the Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI).

%\underline{Market responses are inferred from trends rather than from the mechanisms that produce them.} How a market responds to a change in demand depends on its state (growing, in overcapacity, constrained by resources or scrap), on the size and timing of the change and, for electricity, on when demand occurs. Practice compresses this response into rules applied to market trends: in a growing market, the technologies whose production grows are assumed to supply additional demand.\ci{Weidema2003} Consequential databases apply such rules to projected growth, because running energy-system models consistently for every market is impractical.\ci{vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} Applied to IAM outputs, they give results that are highly sensitive to the time interval chosen and differ substantially between models.\ci{maesProspectiveConsequentialLife2023} Moreover, growth does not show a response to demand: a technology may grow because of mandates or falling costs that would occur regardless of the decision. Energy research derives long-run marginal emission factors from models of capacity expansion,\ci{hawkesLongrunMarginalCO22014,gagnonPlanningEvolutionElectric2022} and early work showed that such analyses and simple rules can point to different marginal technologies.\ci{Mathiesen2009} But this practice is largely limited to electricity, the errors of the rules LCA uses instead have not been measured systematically, and for materials, open models that can be perturbed are scarce.

%% \underline{Technology representations are either generic or built case by case.} A useful representation must capture what the variants of a technology share, so that models are efficient to build and reuse, and how they differ in context, vintage, operating conditions, feedstock and maturity, since consequential questions often turn on these differences: whether an idle old plant or a new one responds, how a process is designed or operated, what an emerging process will look like once deployed.\ci{Majeau-Bettez2021} LCA databases offer breadth without this specificity. Each dataset describes one technology in one setting, is often transferred to other regions with few changes, and rarely distinguishes vintages or operating conditions. Regionalized databases, including our own Regioinvent, adapt the electricity, heat, waste treatment and supply markets a process draws on to its country, not the technology itself.\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} Site-level data show what this hides: across Canadian mining sites producing the same product, our assessment found impacts differing by up to a factor of 13, and by up to a factor of 6 from the proxy datasets normally used.\ci{pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} Parametrized and engineering process models offer specificity without breadth: each is built for one study or sector, with its own conventions, and is rarely designed to connect to others.

%\section{Shared protocol: scenarios, perturbations and tests of validity}

%\textbf{Base scenarios.} Each decision is evaluated against several base scenarios: SSP/RCP pathways from published IAM ensembles,\ci{riahiSharedSocioeconomicPathways2017} completed for metals with stock-and-flow trajectories consistent with the same socioeconomic assumptions (O1.2). The set includes low-demand and sufficiency pathways, which mainstream scenario sets under-represent.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} Scenarios set the market conditions of Pillar I, the boundary conditions of the regional energy model (import and carbon prices) and the context of process models in Pillar II (grid mix, feedstock availability). Results are reported with their robustness across scenarios.

%\textbf{Perturbations.} A decision is represented as a perturbation: a change in demand, design or operation, defined by its size, timing and profile and by whether suppliers can anticipate it, in the same way in every model. Changes in how a process is designed or operated (Pillar II) reach markets (Pillar I) through what they change in demand, such as a load profile or a co-product output.

%\textbf{Tests of validity.} The consequences of a decision cannot be observed directly: they are the difference between two futures, only one of which happens.\ci{oreskesVerificationValidationConfirmation1994} Validity is therefore tested in three ways. Internal consistency is checked in code on every model update: mass and energy balances, which our reconciliation of ecoinvent showed are far from guaranteed in current databases,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} supply equal to demand in each market, and production within capacity. Each simplified representation is compared with a more complete model where one can be run, and with observations where they exist (capacity actually built, plant statistics). Where neither exists, as for undeployed technologies, confidence rests on the errors measured in the first two settings. Both pillars use the same two success criteria: (i) agreement with the reference on the quantities that drive results, measured as the relative error on the impact intensity of the marginal supply mix (Pillar I) or of the main process flows (Pillar II); and (ii) preservation of decision rankings, measured as the rate of pairwise rank reversals across decision alternatives and impact categories. A small deviation that reverses a ranking counts as a failure. Tolerances are set for each case in the protocol, before results are known. The result is an error profile in the settings tested, not a universal threshold.

\section{Pillar I: market consequences}

%\textbf{Concept.} A market consequence is usually summarized by a marginal supplier or mix. We separate it into three questions that can each be tested: how much of a change in demand becomes a change in production, once prices and binding limits such as emission caps adjust; how much of that production comes from new rather than existing capacity, a share that changes over time; and which technologies and suppliers provide it. Current practice addresses mainly the third, assuming that extra demand is met in full by new capacity.\ci{vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} The output is a layer, built on existing open tools (pyam, premise, Brightway), that provides LCA databases with marginal supply mixes per market, documenting for each the scenario and the kind of evidence behind it. Artificial intelligence (AI) serves as a coding aid: it reduces the effort of scripting large batches of model runs and of building audited pipelines for market data, with every value traced to its source.

%\textbf{Approach.} The framework of SO-1 derives the marginal suppliers of every market from the same base scenario, so that all market responses in an LCA follow one transition narrative. Base scenarios are published SSP/RCP pathways,\ci{riahiSharedSocioeconomicPathways2017} including low-demand ones,\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} completed for materials, where IAMs are thin, by trajectories consistent with the same socioeconomic drivers (Task~1.2). Given the state of each market in the scenario (growth or decline, unused capacity), short-term responses are drawn from unused capacity, and long-term responses from changes in the timing of the scenario's capacity additions and retirements, building on our timing-shift method.\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} Because no single method suits all markets, the framework accepts methods of increasing simplification, and records which one was used for each market: perturbation of a structural model where one exists, heuristics applied to published scenario outputs, and market evidence (installed capacity, utilization, announced projects) where neither exists. The tasks measure what each simplification costs on two criteria shared with Pillar~II: agreement with the reference on the impact intensity of the marginal supply, and preservation of the ranking of decision alternatives, with tolerances set for each case before results are known. Tasks~1.1 to~1.3 test the framework against a structural model, then against observations, then extend it where no full reference exists (rows of Figure~1). The framework is released as open software built on existing tools (pyam, premise, Brightway).

\textbf{Task 1.1. Marginal suppliers tested against structural energy models} \emph{(SO-1; PhD~I, years 1--4).} \emph{How large are the errors of CLCA marginal-supplier heuristics relative to a structural model's response, and on what do they depend?} %
%
The thesis first develops the SO-1 framework as consistent open-source software that captures the response of macro-models to perturbations and benchmarks simplifying heuristics against it, yielding scenario-tagged marginal supply mixes with their assumptions recorded. As a first application, we apply perturbations in the energy sector, from marginal to sector-scale in size, under contrasting base scenarios, to at least one global IAM (likely open-source GCAM, and MESSAGE through ongoing collaboration) and to a provincial dynamic ESM (EnergyScope-Quebec). Each model's own response serves as its reference; the two types are chosen for the different mechanisms they expose, not for a direct comparison. We identify how far the response consists of changes in the utilization of existing capacity (short-term effects), in other actors' demand (demand elasticity, captured by the IAMs), or in new capacity (long-term effects). Perturbations also differ in load profile (e.g., winter-peaking heating versus flat industrial demand), testing whether the timing of demand, not only its volume, determines the capacity that responds, a mechanism that EnergyScope-Quebec resolves by peak hour. As macro-models cannot be perturbed in every CLCA, we then benchmark heuristics applied to the same model's baseline at different levels of data availability: our timing-shift method, which brings forward the scenario's planned investments, and trends in capacity or production.


\textbf{Task 1.2. Material markets: hindcast and constrained secondary supply} \emph{(SO-1; MSc~I, years 2--3).} \emph{Would heuristics have identified the metal production capacity actually built, and when does extra demand for recycled metal fall on primary production? }
%
Unlike energy systems, metal markets lack open economic models that can be readily perturbed, but their past capacity additions can be observed. Looking backward, we apply CLCA heuristics to the information available at each date between 2000 and 2020 and compare the suppliers they identify, by vintage, with the capacity actually built (plant trackers, company filings): a falsification test of CLCA heuristics and of their domain of applicability. Looking forward, we perturb a stock-driven MFA model under two or three SSPs to estimate prospective marginal supply mixes, with particular attention to a capacity driver specific to metals: secondary supply constrained by in-use stocks and alloy quality, and thus by sorting rates and the avoidance of inter-alloy contamination, a circular-economy priority. Cases are iron and steel (with ArcelorMittal, a long-standing CIRAIG partner) and aluminium, building on our SSP-consistent dynamic MFA of aluminium alloys.\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} \emph{Outcome:} a historical falsification test of marginal-supplier heuristics, and scenario-consistent trajectories of secondary metal supply for the framework.

\textbf{Task 1.3. From scenario regions to local markets} \emph{(SO-1; MSc~III, years 4--5).} \emph{Does resolving local markets within scenario regions change marginal suppliers enough to change decisions?}
%
IAM scenarios describe large world regions and broad technology classes; a growing regional trend can hide declining local markets. We will first apply the framework of Task~1.1 to fertilizer (nitrogen and phosphorus) markets, as forecasted by global MFAs and IAMs (notably GLOBIOM), to derive the evolution of their marginal suppliers under contrasting scenarios, at their native regional resolution. In these markets, resource concentration (phosphate rock) and trade shape which supply capacity responds; the study builds on our collaboration with OCP, a CIRAIG partner. We will then test disaggregation algorithms (going beyond \textcite{sferraDSCALEV01Opensource2026}) to derive the evolution of local marginal suppliers, including in Canada, from demand-side (land use, demography) and supply-side data (mine and plant capacities, trade, national plans), and compare the results with those at the native regional resolution. \emph{Outcome:} a tested method to derive the evolution of local marginal suppliers from global scenarios, and its sensitivity to disaggregation assumptions.

\textbf{Task 2.1. An interoperability framework for process models} \emph{(SO-2; PhD~II, years 1--4).} \emph{What must a process-model interface expose for models of any sophistication to connect without loss of consistency, and how far do current databases and published models fall short of it?}
%
The thesis first assesses the sophistication and quality control of process parametrization in consequential ecoinvent, a centralized CLCA database: the share of processes with (a) 100\% yields, (b) mass or energy balances that fail to close,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} or (c) one technology identical across regions and vintages. Published reduced engineering models are assessed likewise, with their openness and reusability. We hypothesize a genuine tradeoff between sophistication and flexibility in individual studies and reusability in centralized databases, and that a decentralized interoperability layer can substantially reduce it. The thesis then develops a framework for the \emph{decentralized} publication of interoperable process classes, generalizing the prototype (v0) infrastructure of the 2PURES CFI project on plastic circularity: a compatibility layer wraps technology models of any complexity (fixed inventories, machine-learning surrogates such as XGBoost, phenomenological models) and (a) enforces tests of physical plausibility, (b) separates parameters set by the base scenario (e.g., grid mix, vintage, efficiency frontier) from those set by the decision (design, operation), so that instances derive consistently from macro-scenarios, and (c) exposes the flows needed to connect instances within a p-CLCA system. A central question, long a focus of our group,\ci{Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons,provost-savardSubstitutionModelingCan2024} concerns co-products: when operating conditions change their yields and qualities (e.g., hydrochar from hydrothermal carbonization), the substitutions they induce change too, and the consequential treatment must follow the operating state. We will test the infrastructure by packaging parametrized models from the literature, instantiating them for different geographies and vintages, and connecting the instances to one another and to a consequential database. Success is measured by the share of published models wrapped without loss of functionality and passing the plausibility checks, the share of instances that connect consistently, and the effort relative to conventionally inventorying the same variants; we also quantify the effect of disaggregation on consequential scores. \emph{Outcome:} a general-purpose interface for developing and packaging process models, with documented design principles, tested on published models and through Tasks~2.2 and~2.3.

\textbf{Task 2.2. An efficient workflow, from documented to emerging processes} \emph{(SO-2; PhD~III, years 2--5).} \emph{Can a human-in-the-loop workflow build functional reduced models from publicly accessible material with much less effort, and with what errors? }%
%
Task~2.1 rests on the assumption that, in the long run, it is more efficient to invest in one common, quality-controlled technology model instantiated for different contexts than to inventory each geographical and vintage variant separately; in the short term, however, developing reduced engineering models remains a major investment. We investigate to what extent data collection, process modelling and coding can be automated with generative artificial intelligence (gen-AI) while keeping the resulting code documented and transparent, in a human-in-the-loop workflow: gen-AI drafts model code from first principles and published material, people review it, every parameter is traced to a cited source, and the built-in quality controls of Task~2.1 run as ordinary code. In years 2--3, it is evaluated against reference models (our composting, biomethanization and hydrothermal carbonization models, plus at least one with little public documentation, to bound the advantage of well-documented processes), comparing structure, balance closure, outputs and effort. In years 3--4, it addresses a major uncertainty of low-maturity processes, the flowsheet itself, by assembling alternative configurations from reusable unit-operation modules and design heuristics,\ci{douglasHierarchicalDecisionProcedure1985} with maturity expressed through physical parameters (yields, specific energy) rather than generic learning rates, after checking that it recovers the configuration of a known process. In years 4--5, it is applied to plasma nitrogen fixation as a potential alternative to both fungicides and Haber-Bosch fertilizers, building on ongoing <+PLANET research+>, with configurations evaluated under the electricity and fertilizer-demand contexts of the base scenarios (Tasks~1.1 and~1.3). \emph{Outcome:} an evaluated workflow with its error profile, and a configuration-resolved model of an emerging process.

\textbf{Task 2.3. Reduced models of established, data-rich processes: metallurgy} \emph{(SO-2; MSc~II, years 2--3).} \emph{How far do engineering models of an established process deviate from reported plant performance, and does a calibrated, vintage-resolved model change consequential results relative to generic datasets?} %
%
First-principles reduced models can quickly grow a consistent base of reusable process models and explore novel technologies (Task~2.2), but may miss real-world performance; Task~2.3 tests them against well-documented plant data. It develops a steel class resolved by vintage for one or two routes (blast furnace--basic oxygen furnace; direct reduction--electric arc furnace with natural gas, then hydrogen) from published engineering relations. With ArcelorMittal, it then adds a calibration step to the workflow of Task~2.2, estimating from plant and sector statistics the inefficiencies that design models leave out (part load, start-ups, losses). The calibrated class is instantiated along scenario trajectories (scrap availability from Task~1.2, vintage structure of installed capacity); the efficiency improvements that IAM scenarios assume for the sector are checked against its physical limits; and the class is compared with its uncalibrated version and with ecoinvent datasets, for internal consistency and effect on consequential results. \emph{Outcome:} a calibrated, reusable steel class, a measure of what generic datasets miss, and a physical consistency check of scenario assumptions for steel.

\section{Integration, risks and broader impact}

Integration does not wait for the end of the program: a cumulative table of case results, shared across pillars, is set up in year 1 and maintained by PhD I and PhD II. Energy is a Pillar I case; metallurgy and nitrogen are shared by both pillars (centre of Figure~1). On metallurgy (years 2--3, with ArcelorMittal), Tasks~1.2 and~2.3 test whether distinguishing idle, often old, capacity from new, near-best-available capacity changes which supplier responds and with what flows. On nitrogen (years 4--5), Tasks~1.1, 1.3 and~2.2 ask which matters more for an emerging process: uncertainty about its deployed configuration, or about the market responses it triggers (electricity demanded, fertilizer displaced).

\textbf{Risks and fallbacks.} Each task yields publishable results on its own. If global IAMs prove too costly, Task~1.1 rests on EnergyScope-Quebec and published scenario outputs. Should data or time become limiting, Task~1.2 restricts its scope to a single metal, steel unless its data are inaccessible, in which case Task~2.3 also switches to aluminium. If the AI-assisted workflow underperforms, its measured error profile is itself a result, and classes are built conventionally. If plasma results are too immature, the configuration method is applied to another emerging process from our group.

\textbf{Open infrastructure and its maintenance.} The Pillar~II infrastructure is a decentralized open protocol, not a central database. Interfaces, quality checks and reference implementations are open (versioned releases, continuous integration); parameter sets and instances may be held under other licences (e.g., licensed or confidential data), provided they pass the same checks. Outputs feed maintained tools (Premise, Brightway, Regioinvent, mescal), and ecoinvent-dependent results are regenerated by licensed users. Maintenance of the core is supported by the CIRAIG (International Reference Centre for Life Cycle Assessment and Sustainable Transition), whose industrial chairs have been continuously funded by partners for about 25 years.

\textbf{Partners.} ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec and Québec's Ministère de l'Économie, de l'Innovation et de l'Énergie are full CIRAIG partners, contributing funding, data and engagement. In the metallurgy and energy cases, they help define the decision alternatives to compare, so that the ranking criterion is tested on decisions that matter in practice.

\textbf{Relevance for equity and environmental justice.} Consequential LCA asks not only how large the consequences of a decision are, but where they fall: which mines, smelters or power plants respond, and therefore which communities bear the burdens.\ci{holland2016electricvehicles,owen2023landconnected} Generic datasets, often derived from European or North American contexts, average this distribution away. Our research makes it visible at three levels: marginal suppliers resolved to local markets and, where data allow, to plants (Tasks~1.2 and~1.3);\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025} technology variants resolved by age and emission controls, since a minority of old, poorly controlled units concentrates pollution where these fleets operate;\ci{tong2018superpolluting} and process models recontextualized beyond the high-income regions that dominate LCA data. Results will be reported by region and compared with public spatial data relevant in Canada (income, Indigenous lands, remote regions). They can inform analyses of distributional justice, but do not address procedural justice, which would require community partnerships we do not claim.\ci{jenkins2016energyjustice}

\textbf{Subjective choices in our methods.} Several modelling choices are not neutral; we treat them as explicit assumptions rather than technical defaults. (i) Base scenarios embed assumptions about growth and inequality; we include low-demand pathways, which mainstream sets under-represent.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} (ii) Marginal-supplier methods assume that markets respond through least-cost investment, placing new capacity where production is cheapest, a choice with fairness implications;\ci{pachauri2022fairness} the framework records the assumptions behind each market response. (iii) Engineering literature and AI models are skewed towards high-income regions,\ci{manvi2024llmgeobias} so the evaluation of Task~2.2 includes process instances outside high-income countries where reference data exist, and recontextualized instances carry data-quality flags. (iv) Time horizons and weighting sets can reverse rankings, and preferred weights differ between income groups,\ci{deschryver2013valuechoices,bayazitsubasi2024weighting} so rankings are reported under alternative value sets. Each case study records these choices in a shared assumptions register reviewed with trainees.

\textbf{Significance.} We do not aim to resolve every difficulty of CLCA, but the core limitation that has kept it marginal: its results rest on representations of markets and technologies that are inconsistent with one another and with transition pathways, and whose errors are unknown. Lacking a trusted alternative, decision-makers routinely answer consequential questions with attributional tools, which can mislead them.\ci{Ekvall2004} By deriving market responses and technology representations from the same macro-scale scenarios, this program aims to make p-CLCA consistent and aligned with transition pathways, and to characterize, in contrasting cases, what common simplifications cost. Crucially, it delivers this as a workflow and an open infrastructure that others can adopt and extend: market responses tied to declared scenarios, and process-model classes that the community can instantiate, refine and connect, rather than rebuilding inventories study by study. Its reach would therefore extend well beyond our own cases: if successful, it would change what the LCA community can offer, answering consequential questions with consequential tools at the resolution of individual design, operation and deployment decisions. For Canadian decision-makers in metallurgy, fertilizer and electricity, it will provide a way to test whether such choices support the transition pathways they plan for. Its HQP will combine LCA, system models, process modelling and verified data pipelines.
