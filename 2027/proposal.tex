\noindent \textbf{Description of the Research Program}

\begin{markdown}

Context and research gap
========================

Energy and climate policies are largely guided by energy-system models (ESMs) and integrated assessment models (IAMs),\ci{Pauliuk2017} run on shared, internally consistent futures: the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) and Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs).\ci{oneillNewScenarioFramework2014} These models are too coarse for the thousands of micro- and meso-level decisions through which transitions actually happen: which technology a firm adopts, how a plant is designed and operated, which process is scaled up. Such decisions are informed by life-cycle assessment (LCA),\ci{Hellweg2014,hellwegLifecycleAssessmentGuide2023} mostly in its attributional form, which describes the share of present-day impacts associated with a product. Prospective databases such as premise now align LCA process data (inventories) with IAM scenarios,\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} which makes them temporally consistent, but they remain attributional: they describe a product's share of a future economy, not how that economy changes in response to the decision under study.\ci{Heijungs2001,Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons}

Two examples show why this matters. First, an industry supplied by clean hydroelectricity sees little benefit in saving electricity in a prospective attributional LCA, whereas energy-system models identify such savings as essential to free clean capacity for other sectors. What counts is the change in capacity built and operated, and since capacity follows peak demand rather than annual energy, the same kilowatt-hours saved can have very different consequences depending on *when* they are saved. Second, an attributional LCA rewards recycled content. Yet when scrap supply is constrained by in-use stocks, extra demand for recycled metal only diverts scrap from other uses, and the actual response is extra primary production.\ci{Pauliuk2015TakingStock} Whether scrap is constrained depends on stock maturity, and thus on region and time.

Consequential LCA (CLCA) was developed to capture such effects: it quantifies how environmental flows change when a decision perturbs the economy.\ci{Ekvall2004,Zamagni2012} Combined with scenario-consistent futures, prospective CLCA (p-CLCA) is the natural tool to align micro-level decisions with macro-level transition pathways. The case for p-CLCA is well established; the obstacle is *how* to compute it credibly. We see two gaps.

**Gap 1: market consequences are read from trends rather than derived from their causes.** The marginal suppliers that respond to a change in demand are mostly identified with trend heuristics: technologies whose production grows are assumed to respond.\ci{Weidema2003} Applied to IAM outputs, this gives results that are highly sensitive to the time interval chosen and differ substantially between IAMs.\ci{maesProspectiveConsequentialLife2023} Trend rules also confuse growth that responds to demand with growth set by the calendar (mandates, carbon-price paths, exogenous cost declines); only the first is a consequence of extra demand. More fundamentally, the standard long-term assumption bundles three untested links: extra demand becomes extra supply, that supply comes from new rather than existing capacity, and some technology supplies it. Most methodological effort goes into the last link. Models that could test all three exist, but perturbing them for each LCA was long judged too time-consuming.\ci{vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} Coverage is also uneven: energy has open models that can be perturbed; materials mostly do not.

**Gap 2: processes are represented as fixed recipes.** Even a correctly identified marginal supplier is then represented by a generic inventory: datasets in reference databases such as ecoinvent are often cloned across geographies, assume idealized yields, and represent a single vintage of technology.\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026,agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} When all candidate suppliers map onto the same proxy, marginal-supplier analysis produces precise-looking but empty distinctions. Fixed recipes also cannot represent decisions about *how* a process is designed or operated (operating conditions, feedstock, co-product output), nor anticipate what an emerging process will look like once deployed, which is often more uncertain than the choice of its suppliers.

Finally, CLCAs are usually defined relative to a single baseline in which the status quo prevails.\ci{Steubing2016} In a transition, the consequence of a decision depends on the state of the economy: whether a market is growing or in overcapacity, whether scrap is scarce or abundant. Robust guidance requires evaluating each decision as a perturbation around several internally consistent base scenarios.


Long-term goal, research questions and approach
===============================================

**Long-term goal.** Our long-term goal is to make p-CLCA rest on the *perturbation of models*, anchored in transition scenarios, rather than on fixed recipes and trend extrapolation, so that LCA can guide which technologies to deploy *and* how to design and operate them. Beyond this grant, we aim at an open ecosystem of interoperable process-model classes and market-response layers, maintained by a community as open-source software is. We do not try to outdo economists at market modelling, nor process engineers at process modelling. Our contribution is pragmatic, and three elements of it are new: a decomposition of market consequences into links that can each be tested against a perturbed model; process models designed as interoperable classes whose interfaces carry the causal links consequential LCA needs; and a shared evaluation protocol that characterizes the error of each shortcut, so that practitioners know what a simplification costs them. Each objective builds on recent results of our group, cited where they are used.

The program has two pillars (Figure 1), carried by six highly qualified personnel (HQP). **Pillar I (market consequences)** asks *which units operate*: which suppliers respond to a decision, when, and where. **Pillar II (process consequences)** asks *how they operate*: how processes and their environmental flows respond to design, operation, context and maturity. Both pillars address engineering questions of *how*, made decidable by explicit success criteria rather than by tests of general laws:

- **RQ-I.** How can the market consequences of decisions be represented consistently in p-CLCA from models of different types and resolutions (from structural perturbation of models to heuristics applied to published scenarios and, where no model exists, to market evidence), and disaggregated to the local markets where decisions play out?
- **RQ-II.** How can technologies be represented as parametrized process models that capture the consequences of changes in design, operation and deployment context, and how can such models be built, evaluated and reduced efficiently?
- **Program-level question.** How much do representation choices change the conclusions of p-CLCA across decision types, at the market level (which units respond) and at the process level (how they operate)?

Shared protocol: base scenarios, perturbations and evaluation
=============================================================

**Base scenarios.** Each decision is evaluated as a perturbation around several base scenarios: SSP/RCP pathways from published IAM ensembles,\ci{riahiSharedSocioeconomicPathways2017} completed with material trajectories consistent with the same SSP drivers (stock-driven dynamic material flow analysis, MFA; O1.3). The set deliberately includes low-demand and sufficiency pathways, which mainstream SSP sets under-represent.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} Scenarios enter Pillar I as market regimes and capacity flows, the regional energy-system model as boundary conditions (import prices, carbon-price paths), and Pillar II as the context parameters of process-model instances (grid mix, feedstock availability, year). Conclusions are reported with their robustness across base scenarios.

**Perturbations.** A perturbation is a change in a decision variable, characterized by its size, start, end, time profile and information lead (when suppliers learn of it). The same definition is used in every model. Process-level changes studied in Pillar II are passed to Pillar I through their effect on demand, such as a changed load profile or co-product output.

**Evaluation, not validation.** The consequences of a decision are counterfactual and cannot be observed directly,\ci{oreskesVerificationValidationConfirmation1994} so credibility rests on two layers. First, internal consistency is verified in code on every model update: mass and energy balances,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} supply equal to demand in each market, production within capacity, capacity changes equal to additions minus retirements. Second, each simplified representation is compared with a reference of decreasing strength (rows of Figure 1): a structural model that we can perturb, whose own response is the reference for shortcuts applied to its baseline; observed or reported data (capacity actually built, plant statistics); and, where no full reference exists (local markets, undeployed technologies), the error profiles established on known cases. Both pillars use the same **two success criteria**: (i) agreement with the reference on the quantities that drive results, measured as the relative error on the impact intensity of the marginal mix (Pillar I) or on major process flows (Pillar II); and (ii) preservation of decision rankings, measured as the rate of pairwise rank reversals across decision alternatives and impact categories. A small deviation that flips a ranking counts as a failure. Tolerances are set for each case in the protocol, before results are known. The output is an error profile in the settings tested, not a universal threshold, and each completed case adds a row to a cumulative comparison table.


Pillar I: market consequences
=============================

**Concept.** We decompose a market consequence into three explicit links, each with its own parameter. The *net demand change* $\nu$ is the share of a demand shock that becomes extra supply after price feedback and binding caps. The *capacity response share* $\varphi(t)$ is the share of that extra supply met by new rather than existing capacity; it rises from zero over time and depends on the market regime in the base scenario (growing or in overcapacity), the duration of the perturbation relative to lead times, its information lead, institutions (a planned utility builds to forecast peak; an energy-only market responds to scarcity prices) and, for electricity, the load profile. The *technology mix* $m(t)$ describes which suppliers respond. The standard long-term assumption sets $\nu = \varphi = 1$ and debates only $m$.

**Tiers.** Each sector is handled at the most structural tier its models allow, and each tier is defined by its reference: **Tier A** perturbs an open model, whose response is the reference for heuristics applied to that model's own baseline (no official calibration is needed); **Tier B** uses published scenario outputs, disaggregated to countries and reconciled with existing fleets, trade and planned projects, evaluated against Tier A error profiles and inter-model spread; **Tier C** uses market evidence (project pipelines, utilization, trade) where no model exists, evaluated by hindcasting. The output is a layer, built on existing tools (pyam, premise, Brightway), that turns scenarios into marginal mixes per market and per tier, with metadata on regime, $\varphi$ and error bounds. Artificial intelligence (AI) serves here as a coding aid for perturbation batches and audited data pipelines: by lowering the cost that long justified trend heuristics, it moves the boundary between Tiers A and B.

**O1.1 Representation of market consequences, tested in perturbable models** *(PhD I, years 1--4).* PhD I formalizes $\nu$, $\varphi$ and $m$ and the accounting that turns a model's response into marginal mixes, and decomposes IAM capacity growth into demand-driven and calendar-driven parts, which to our knowledge has not been done. The first work package uses EnergyScope Québec,\ci{limpensEnergyScopePathwayOpensource2024,limpensEnergyScopeTDNovel2019,souttreMescalToolCoupling2025} an open model with peak-hour resolution that our group has calibrated and coupled to LCA. Perfect-foresight and myopic versions test the foresight assumption; peak-coincident and flat perturbations (a winter-peaking heat-pump load against a flat industrial load) test the load-profile effect; industrial heat is handled with a linked PhD on industrial energy use with CanmetENERGY. Against these structural responses, we benchmark the trend heuristic and the timing-shift heuristic developed with Provost-Savard,\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} in which a marginal demand is first met by unused capacity and then pulls forward the next capacity additions of the scenario, including the effects that persist after the perturbation ends. In years 3--4, the experiments are repeated in a global model (GCAM or MESSAGEix, selected in year 2) for energy and nitrogen fertilizer, to compare error profiles across contrasting system types and model structures; contrasts within each model (e.g., Canada against other regions) help separate the two.

**O1.2 From regional scenarios to local markets** *(MSc III, years 4--5).* IAM trends are given for aggregate regions and broad technology classes; a growing regional trend can hide declining countries, so local marginal suppliers can differ from regional ones. MSc III disaggregates regional trends to countries and reconciles them with plant-level fleets, trade links and national plans (Tier B), for the North American nitrogen fertilizer and ammonia market, and checks the result against the global-model perturbations of O1.1.

**O1.3 Metallurgy: hindcast and scenario-consistent trajectories** *(MSc I, years 2--3).* Backward, MSc I applies heuristic marginals to the data available at each time between 2000 and 2020 and compares them with the capacity actually built (Tier C), using audited extraction pipelines from plant trackers and company filings. Forward, the reconstructed market is extended into a stock-driven dynamic MFA under two or three SSPs, building on our SSP-consistent aluminium model and metal–energy scenarios.\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022,bieuvilleMetalBottlenecksEnergy2025} Perturbing it shows when scrap is constrained, and therefore when extra demand falls on primary routes; price responses ($\nu$) are outside its scope. Steel comes first; aluminium, for which our models and data already exist, is the fallback.


Pillar II: process consequences
===============================

**Concept.** Pillar II represents technologies as *process-model classes*: for instance, a steel-plant class that can be instantiated for Québec or China, today or in 2050, by vintage and operating state. Internals can vary in detail, but interfaces are consistent and quality checks are built in (mass, energy and elemental balances, validity ranges, regression tests). Instances compile into existing formats (EcoSpold2, Brightway): the model package is the source, the database is a compiled product. Process-systems engineering already couples process models with LCA, mostly in attributional settings with a fixed background.\ci{Azapagic1999} Our focus is to retain the causal links (co-products, feedstock competition, load profiles) through which design and operation decisions propagate to the rest of the economy. A prototype (v0), implemented with software-development support from the Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI), embodies provisional design choices that this program evaluates and revises.

**O2.1 Representation of process-model classes for consequential LCA** *(PhD II, years 1--4).* PhD II starts with an automated diagnostic of ecoinvent: unit yields, balances that fail to close where composition is known, geographic clones, and the vintages represented. Regionalized databases such as Regioinvent regionalize the supply mixes a process draws on, not the technology of the process itself.\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} A global sensitivity analysis on representative consequential systems\ci{kaddouraGlobalSensitivityAnalysis2025} then ranks the processes whose variants could change results, which bounds the scope. The thesis then addresses the design of the class interface: which context dimensions (geography, vintage, maturity, operating state, feedstock) it exposes and how they propagate to connected classes; how co-products and their consequential treatment are exposed, a long-standing strength of our group;\ci{provost-savardSubstitutionModelingCan2024} structural uncertainty as alternative flowsheets; maturity as explicit physical parameters (yield, specific energy, catalyst life) with sourced priors rather than generic learning curves; reduction to surrogates that preserve decision-relevant behaviour; and what a confidential instance must expose to remain checkable. The output is an evaluation of v0 and a revised interface (v1), tested through O2.2 and O2.3.

**O2.2 AI-assisted construction and anticipation, from known to undeployed processes** *(PhD III, years 2--5).* PhD III develops a human-in-the-loop workflow in which AI, mobilizing broad engineering knowledge, helps build functional reduced models, as tested code on the O2.1 interface, from first principles and publicly accessible material. AI writes code that people review; checks run as ordinary code; no data are generated by the AI. In years 2--3, it is evaluated where the answer is known, comparing structure, balance closure, outputs and effort against reference models: our published composting and hydrothermal carbonization models,\ci{ravoahangyParametrizationBiowasteComposting2026,tanguay-riouxCanHydrothermalCarbonization2026} plus at least one with little public documentation, to bound the advantage of well-documented processes. In years 3--4, the workflow addresses the dominant uncertainty of low-maturity processes, flowsheet structure, by generating alternative flowsheets from reusable unit-operation modules and design heuristics,\ci{douglasHierarchicalDecisionProcedure1985} first recovering the configuration of a known process. In years 4--5, it is applied to an undeployed process: plasma nitrogen fixation as a potential replacement for Haber-Bosch ammonia, building on the results of an ongoing collaborative project (PLASMA, ending in 2027).\ci{anastasopoulouEcoefficiencyAnalysisPlasmaassisted2020}

**O2.3 Metallurgy class, benchmarked against existing data** *(MSc II, years 2--3).* MSc II builds a steel class resolved by vintage for one or two routes (blast furnace–basic oxygen furnace; direct reduction–electric arc furnace with natural gas, then hydrogen). The class is calibrated against plant and sector statistics to capture the inefficiencies that design models leave out (part load, start-ups, losses), and compared with ecoinvent datasets for internal consistency and for its effect on downstream consequential results. Starting before v1, it is the first real-world stress test of v0. Paired with O1.3, it gives substance to the distinction between short-term (idle, often old) and long-term (new, near best-available) marginal capacity.


Integration, timeline and risks
===============================

Integration is built in, not deferred: the shared protocol (Section 3) and comparison table exist from year 1, maintained jointly by PhD I and PhD II. Cases sit at the centre of Figure 1. *Energy* (Pillar I) uses EnergyScope Québec, including industrial heat. Two cases are shared by both pillars. *Metallurgy*: MSc I and MSc II work on steel at the same time (years 2--3), with ArcelorMittal. *Nitrogen*: MSc III and PhD III work at the same time (years 4--5) and test a joint hypothesis by variance decomposition, namely that uncertainty about the deployed configuration of an emerging process exceeds uncertainty about its background marginal suppliers. Two synthesis moments led by the applicant answer the program-level question: at mid-term (year 3), from the metallurgy pair, and in year 5, co-authored with the PhD students.

**Risks and fallbacks.** Each objective yields publishable results on its own. If the global model proves too costly to operate, EnergyScope Québec delivers the core of O1.1 and the comparison relies on published scenario outputs. If steel data are inaccessible, O1.3 and O2.3 switch to aluminium. If the AI-assisted workflow underperforms, its characterized error profile is itself the result. If plasma results are too immature, the flowsheet-alternative method is applied to another emerging process from our group's work.


Open infrastructure, partners, equity and significance
======================================================

**Open infrastructure and its maintenance.** We are not building a new central database but an open, decentralizable protocol: class interfaces, quality checks and reference implementations are open (versioned releases, continuous integration), while parameter sets and instances can be held under different licences (licensed databases, confidential company data exported as aggregated inventories), provided they pass the same checks. Results derived from ecoinvent are regenerated by licensed users, and outputs feed tools that are already maintained (premise, Brightway, Regioinvent, mescal). Stewardship of the core rests with the CIRAIG (International Reference Centre for Life Cycle Assessment and Sustainable Transition), whose industrial research chairs have been funded continuously by partners for about 25 years.

**Partners.** Rather than through a generic panel, stakeholders are involved through cases. ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec and Québec's Ministère de l'Économie, de l'Innovation et de l'Énergie are full CIRAIG partners, contributing funding, data and engagement. For the metallurgy and energy cases, they help define the decision alternatives and the rankings that the second success criterion tests.

**Equity, diversity and inclusion in the research.** A p-CLCA determines which suppliers, and therefore which places, respond to a decision: the communities that host marginal mines, smelters or power plants are an output of our models.\ci{holland2016electricvehicles,owen2023landconnected} The geographic resolution of Pillar I and our site-level metal data\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025,pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} allow results to be reported by region and, where public spatial data exist, overlaid with characteristics relevant in Canada (income, Indigenous lands, remote regions). This addresses the distribution of burdens, not procedural justice, which would require community partnerships we do not claim. Value choices also matter: time horizons and weighting sets can reverse rankings, and preferred weights differ between income groups.\ci{deschryver2013valuechoices,bayazitsubasi2024weighting} The ranking criterion is therefore reported across alternative value sets. Finally, because AI models and the engineering literature are skewed towards high-income regions,\ci{manvi2024llmgeobias} the evaluation of O2.2 includes process instances from lower-income regions.

**Significance.** The program will give LCA practitioners consequential results with characterized errors, and Canadian decision-makers in metallurgy, fertilizer and electricity a way to test whether a design, operating or deployment choice supports the transition pathways they plan for. Its HQP will be trained across LCA, system models, process modelling and audited data pipelines.
