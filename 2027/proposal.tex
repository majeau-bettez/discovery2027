\noindent \textbf{Description of the Research Program}

\section{Context, research gap, and opportunity}

National decarbonization policies and international climate agreements are largely guided by energy system models (ESMs) and integrated assessment models (IAMs),\ci{Pauliuk2017} whose community has converged on a small set of internally consistent socioeconomic and mitigation pathways: the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) and Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs).\ci{oneillNewScenarioFramework2014} Similarly, circular-economy roadmaps rest on macro-scale material flow analyses (MFAs).\ci{aistRoadmapIronSteel2025,alonsoWorldMineralsOutlook,canadaplasticspactRoadmap2025Shared2021} These tools guide economy-wide policy, but are too coarse to inform the thousands of decisions by consumers, firms and sectors that implement transitions.

These micro- and meso-scale decisions are instead increasingly guided by attributional life-cycle assessment (ALCA), which estimates the share of environmental impacts linked to a product or function, as in carbon footprints and environmental product declarations.\ci{Hellweg2014,hellwegLifecycleAssessmentGuide2023} Most ALCAs are \emph{retrospective}, relying on past data, which limits their use for forward-looking decisions. Prospective ALCA (p-ALCA) databases such as Premise now incorporate macro-scenario data,\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} bringing temporal consistency to the assessment of long-lived products and investments.

Major inconsistencies persist, however, between micro-scale p-ALCAs and macro-scale transition models. An industry already supplied by clean electricity will see, in its p-ALCA, little benefit in further improving its energy efficiency; yet energy system models often deem such gains essential to free clean electricity capacity for other sectors. What appears negligible in a static footprint can thus be critical in the dynamic transition context. Material cycles show the same problem: a p-ALCA may recommend maximizing the recycled content of a product, while dynamic MFAs show that limited in-use stocks will constrain scrap supply for years,\ci{Pauliuk2015TakingStock} so that more recycled content in one product may simply divert scarce secondary material from another. Both contradictions arise because p-ALCAs remain \emph{attributional}: limited to the value chains under study, they cannot capture the market-mediated, system-wide consequences that shape the transition.\ci{Heijungs2001,Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons}

Capturing such mechanisms has long been the aim of consequential LCA (CLCA):\ci{Zamagni2012} whereas ALCAs describe how impacts are distributed within the value chains of a given economy, CLCAs quantify how impacts change when a decision perturbs the economy, its \emph{consequences}.\ci{Ekvall2004} Yet, though theorized in the early 2000s, CLCA remains a niche approach.\ci{bamberComparingSourcesAnalysis2020} It requires identifying which suppliers would respond to a change in demand for each product (marginal suppliers),\ci{Weidema2003} which is highly uncertain and context-dependent.\ci{Mathiesen2009} Guidelines provide rules for this identification, and consequential databases have attempted to apply them across the economy,\ci{Weidema2003,vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} but with heterogeneous assumptions and little internal consistency; extensions of these rules to transition scenarios are only recent.\ci{maesProspectiveConsequentialLife2023,provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} These rules rely on market trends and scarce empirical data, and detailed modelling is often restricted to a few processes in the foreground of the product system, inconsistently with the background database. Building CLCAs has thus proven costly, and their results, resting on simplifications whose errors are largely unknown, are often hard to interpret and viewed with skepticism. Finally, CLCAs are almost always defined against a single scenario, the \emph{status quo} over the lifetime of the decision,\ci{Steubing2016} an assumption ill-suited to investment and production decisions in a transitioning economy.

We see a \textbf{strategic opportunity} to make prospective CLCA (p-CLCA) a systematic bridge between macro-scale models (ESMs, IAMs, MFAs) and micro- and meso-scale decisions, anchoring each analysis as a perturbation of broader representative scenarios so that LCA guidance aligns with transition pathways.

\section{Core challenges and objectives}

We address two core challenges of CLCA through a common strategy. The first (\textbf{Pillar I}) is to represent how markets respond to marginal changes in demand. Sophisticated economic models would ideally capture this, but cannot be run for the thousands of markets in a CLCA, which are instead represented by simplifying heuristics. These heuristics are currently (a) not bound by a consistent narrative, (b) of widely varying sophistication, with uncharacterized errors, and (c) inferred from trends rather than from the mechanisms that drive capacity investment (e.g., the timing of demand relative to peak in electricity systems). Our objective (\textbf{O-1}) is therefore a scenario-consistent framework for identifying marginal suppliers: the responses of all markets in an assessment are derived from the same base scenario, by perturbing a model or applying heuristics to its outputs, informed by these mechanisms, and the errors of each level of simplification are characterized.

The second challenge (\textbf{Pillar II}) is to represent how technological processes respond to design and operating decisions and to deployment context. Sophisticated engineering models would ideally capture this, but cannot be built for the thousands of processes in a CLCA, which are instead represented with little parametrization or as fixed black boxes; prospective databases make these evolve along macro-scenarios by rescaling inputs and emissions, aligning them with the scenario but not necessarily with process physics.\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} These simplifications (a) prevent the assessment of design and operating decisions and (b) obscure differences between technology variants by location, context and age; identifying the right marginal supplier (Pillar~I) is of little use if all suppliers share the same recipe. They also (c) make LCA work inefficient: detailed engineering models, where used, are rarely formatted for reuse or interconnection, limiting collaboration on common models and their adaptation to new contexts. Our objective (\textbf{O-2}) is therefore twofold. First, an interoperability framework that accommodates process models of any sophistication and represents technologies as instances of broader \emph{process-model classes}, parametrized by the base scenario for their context and technology evolution, and by the decision under study for their design and operation. Second, an efficient workflow to populate these classes with reduced engineering models, from established to emerging ones.

In both pillars, our \textbf{common strategy} is to (a) mirror each conceptual framework in open-source software, to support a change in community practice: an integration framework for market responses, as an open layer on existing tools (Pillar~I), and a modelling framework for process-model classes, as a new open digital infrastructure (Pillar~II); (b) resolve what macro-scenarios aggregate ---broad regions and technology classes--- by representing the markets and processes needed in CLCA as instances of broader ones, all anchored in the same base scenario; (c) accommodate simplifying assumptions consistently, to fit differing data availability and resolution; and (d) characterize the errors of these simplifications against references of decreasing strength (rows of Figure~\ref{fig:map}): models whose response is known by construction (Tasks~1.1, 2.1 and~2.2), then observed and reported data (built capacity, Task~1.2; plant performance, Task~2.3), and, where no full reference exists, the sensitivity of rankings to alternative representations (Task~1.3; emerging processes, Task~2.2). We pursue these through case studies, each evaluated under at least two contrasting SSP/RCP base scenarios, including a low-demand pathway.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025} This breadth is deliberate: a framework meant for the many markets of a CLCA must hold across sectors with different drivers of market and process response. Across our cases, we ask a \textbf{program-level question}: under which conditions do these representation choices reverse the ranking of the options in a decision by their potential environmental consequences? Each case compares concrete options, and a choice is deemed decisive when it reverses the ranking of two options on climate change or another indicator declared for the case, beyond result uncertainty, under at least one base scenario.

These short-term objectives contribute to our \textbf{long-term goal}: a p-CLCA practice built on shared, open representations of markets and technologies, maintained and extended by the community, that provides pragmatic, discriminating decision support for consumption, process design and operation in a world in transition.

\textbf{Recent progress.} For market responses, we developed guidelines and a timing-shift method for deriving marginal suppliers from IAM scenarios,\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} calibrated the open-source ESM EnergyScope-Quebec, and integrated LCA metrics in ESM optimization.\ci{souttreMescalToolCoupling2025} For databases, we regionalized ecoinvent with trade data\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} and pushed forward its reconciliation with mass conservation.\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} For material flows, we built SSP-consistent aluminium scenarios,\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} quantified metal bottlenecks along IAM energy-transition scenarios,\ci{bieuvilleMetalBottlenecksEnergy2025} and assembled site-level databases on Canadian metal production.\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025,pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} For processes, we developed highly parametrized models of composting and hydrothermal carbonization.\ci{ravoahangyParametrizationBiowasteComposting2026,tanguay-riouxCanHydrothermalCarbonization2026} A prototype infrastructure for process model integration (v0) is being implemented with software-development support from the Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI).

\section{Pillar I: market consequences}

\textbf{Task 1.1. Marginal suppliers tested against structural energy models} \emph{(O-1; PhD~I, years 1--4).} \emph{How large are the errors of CLCA marginal-supplier heuristics relative to a structural model's response, and on what do they depend?} %
%
The thesis first formalizes the perturbation protocol of O-1 and implements it in open-source software that captures the response of macro-models to perturbations and benchmarks simplifying heuristics against it, yielding scenario-tagged marginal supply mixes with their assumptions recorded. As a first application, we apply perturbations in the energy sector, from marginal to sector-scale in size, under contrasting base scenarios, to the global IAM MESSAGE-GLOBIOM, through our ongoing collaboration with IIASA, and to a provincial dynamic ESM (EnergyScope-Quebec). Each model's own response serves as its reference; the two types are chosen for the different mechanisms they expose, not for a direct comparison. We identify how far the response consists of changes in the utilization of existing capacity (short-term effects), in other actors' demand (demand elasticity, captured by the IAMs), or in new capacity (long-term effects). Perturbations also differ in load profile (e.g., winter-peaking heating versus flat industrial demand), testing whether the timing of demand, not only its volume, determines the capacity that responds, a mechanism that EnergyScope-Quebec resolves by peak hour. As macro-models cannot be perturbed in every CLCA, we then benchmark heuristics applied to the same model's baseline at different levels of data availability: our timing-shift method, which brings forward the scenario's planned investments, and trends in capacity or production. Errors are measured on the marginal mix (technology shares), on the per-unit impacts of marginal supply, and on rank reversals in the case decisions.

\textbf{Task 1.2. Steel markets: hindcast and constrained secondary supply} \emph{(O-1; MSc~I, years 2--3).} \emph{Would heuristics have identified the steel production capacity actually built, and when does extra demand for recycled steel fall on primary production?}
%
Unlike energy systems, metal markets lack open economic models that can be readily perturbed, but their past capacity additions can be observed. Looking backward, we apply CLCA heuristics to the information available at each date between 2000 and 2020 and compare the suppliers they identify, by vintage, with the capacity actually built (plant trackers, company filings): a falsification test of CLCA heuristics and of their domain of applicability. Looking forward, we perturb a stock-driven MFA model under two or three SSPs to estimate prospective marginal supply mixes, with particular attention to a capacity driver specific to metals: secondary supply constrained by in-use stocks and scrap quality (e.g., copper contamination), and thus by sorting rates, a circular-economy priority. The case is iron and steel, shared with Task~2.3 and with ArcelorMittal, a long-standing CIRAIG partner. \emph{Outcome:} a historical falsification test of marginal-supplier heuristics, and scenario-consistent trajectories of secondary steel supply.

\textbf{Task 1.3. From scenario regions to local markets} \emph{(O-1; MSc~III, years 4--5).} \emph{Does resolving local markets within scenario regions change marginal suppliers enough to change decisions?}
%
IAM scenarios describe large world regions and broad technology classes; a growing regional trend can hide declining local markets. We will first apply the framework of Task~1.1 to fertilizer (nitrogen and phosphorus) markets, as forecasted by global MFAs and IAMs (notably GLOBIOM), to derive the evolution of their marginal suppliers under contrasting scenarios, at their native regional resolution. In these markets, resource concentration (phosphate rock) and trade shape which supply capacity responds; the study builds on our collaboration with OCP, a CIRAIG partner. We will then test disaggregation algorithms (going beyond \textcite{sferraDSCALEV01Opensource2026}) to derive the evolution of local marginal suppliers, including in Canada, from demand-side (land use, demography) and supply-side data (mine and plant capacities, trade, national plans), and compare the results with those at the native regional resolution. \emph{Outcome:} a tested method to derive the evolution of local marginal suppliers from global scenarios, and its sensitivity to disaggregation assumptions.

\textbf{Task 2.1. An interoperability framework to consolidate process knowledge for CLCA} \emph{(O-2; PhD~II, years 1--4).} \emph{What must a process-model interface expose for models of any sophistication to connect without loss of consistency, and how far do current databases and published models fall short of it?}
%
The thesis first assesses the sophistication and quality control of process parametrization in consequential ecoinvent, a centralized CLCA database: the share of processes with (a) 100\% yields, (b) mass or energy balances that fail to close,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} or (c) one technology identical across regions and vintages. Published reduced engineering models are assessed likewise, with their openness and reusability. We hypothesize a genuine tradeoff between sophistication and flexibility in individual studies and reusability in centralized databases, and that a decentralized interoperability layer can substantially reduce it. The thesis then develops a framework for the \emph{decentralized} publication of interoperable process classes, generalizing the prototype (v0) infrastructure of the 2PURES CFI project on plastic circularity: a compatibility layer wraps technology models of any complexity (fixed inventories, machine-learning surrogates such as XGBoost, phenomenological models) and (a) enforces tests of physical plausibility, (b) separates parameters set by the base scenario (e.g., grid mix, vintage, efficiency frontier) from those set by the decision (design, operation), so that instances are consistent with macro-scenarios, and (c) exposes the flows needed to connect instances within a p-CLCA system. A central question, long a focus of our group,\ci{Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons,provost-savardSubstitutionModelingCan2024} concerns co-products: when operating conditions change their yields and qualities (e.g., hydrochar from hydrothermal carbonization), the substitutions they induce change too, and the consequential treatment must follow the operating state. We will test the infrastructure by packaging parametrized models from the literature, instantiating them for different geographies and vintages, and connecting the instances to one another and to a consequential database. Success is measured by the share of published models wrapped without loss of functionality and passing the plausibility checks, the share of instances that connect consistently, and the effort relative to conventionally inventorying the same variants; we also quantify the effect of disaggregation on consequential scores. \emph{Outcome:} an interoperability framework in support of a decentralized workflow built on quality-checked process classes (design principles and an open interface), with evidence on its fidelity, consistency and effort relative to conventional inventories, from published models and Tasks~2.2--2.3.

\textbf{Task 2.2. An efficient workflow, from documented to emerging processes} \emph{(O-2; PhD~III, years 2--5).} \emph{Can a human-in-the-loop workflow build functional reduced models from publicly accessible material with much less effort, and with what errors? }%
%
Task~2.1 rests on the assumption that, in the long run, it is more efficient to invest in one common, quality-controlled technology model instantiated for different contexts than to inventory each geographical and vintage variant separately; in the short term, however, developing reduced engineering models remains a major investment. We investigate to what extent data collection, process modelling and coding can be automated with generative artificial intelligence (gen-AI) while keeping the resulting code documented and transparent, in a human-in-the-loop workflow: gen-AI drafts model code from first principles and published material, people review it, every parameter is traced to a cited source, and the built-in quality controls of Task~2.1 run as ordinary code. In years 2--3, it is evaluated against reference models (our composting, biomethanization and hydrothermal carbonization models, plus at least one with little public documentation, to bound the advantage of well-documented processes), comparing structure, balance closure, outputs and effort; it also drafts a vintage-resolved steel class from published engineering relations, which Task~2.3 confronts with plant data. In years 3--4, it addresses a major uncertainty of low-maturity processes, the flowsheet itself, by assembling alternative configurations from reusable unit-operation modules and design heuristics,\ci{douglasHierarchicalDecisionProcedure1985} with maturity expressed through physical parameters (yields, specific energy) rather than generic learning rates, after checking that it recovers the configuration of a known process. In years 4--5, it is applied to plasma nitrogen fixation as a potential alternative to both fungicides and Haber-Bosch fertilizers, building on the ongoing PLANET project (NSERC-SSHRC Sustainable agriculture research initiative), with configurations evaluated under the electricity and fertilizer-demand contexts of the base scenarios (Tasks~1.1 and~1.3). \emph{Outcome:} an evaluated workflow with its error profile, and a configuration-resolved model of an emerging process.

\textbf{Task 2.3. Calibrating reduced models of established processes: steel} \emph{(O-2; MSc~II, years 2--3).} \emph{How far do engineering models of an established process deviate from reported plant performance, and does a calibrated, vintage-resolved model change consequential results relative to generic datasets?} %
%
First-principles reduced models (Task~2.2) can quickly grow a base of reusable classes but may miss real-world performance; Task~2.3 tests them against well-documented plant data. It takes the steel class drafted in Task~2.2 (blast furnace--basic oxygen furnace; direct reduction--electric arc furnace with natural gas, then hydrogen), assembles with ArcelorMittal reference plant and sector data, and adds a calibration step to the workflow, estimating the inefficiencies that design models leave out (part load, start-ups, losses). The calibrated class is instantiated along scenario trajectories (scrap availability from Task~1.2, vintage structure of installed capacity); the efficiency improvements that IAM scenarios assume for the sector are checked against its physical limits; and the class is compared with its uncalibrated version and with ecoinvent datasets, for internal consistency and effect on consequential results. \emph{Outcome:} a calibrated, reusable steel class, a measure of what generic datasets miss, and a physical consistency check of scenario assumptions for steel.

\section{Integration, risks and broader impact}

Integration does not wait for the end of the program: a cumulative table of case results (options, representation choices, rank reversals), shared across pillars, is set up in year 1 and maintained by PhD I and PhD II. Energy is a Pillar I case; metallurgy and fertilizers are shared by both pillars (centre of Figure~\ref{fig:map}). On metallurgy (years 2--3, with ArcelorMittal), Tasks~1.2, 2.2 and~2.3 test whether distinguishing idle, often old, capacity from new, near-best-available capacity changes which supplier responds and with what flows. On fertilizers (years 4--5), Tasks~1.1, 1.3 and~2.2 ask which matters more for an emerging process: uncertainty about its deployed configuration, or about the market responses it triggers (electricity demanded, fertilizer displaced).
\textbf{Risks and fallbacks.} Each task yields publishable results on its own. If perturbing MESSAGE-GLOBIOM proves too costly, Tasks~1.1 and~1.3 fall back on the open-source IAM GCAM. Should steel data prove inaccessible, Tasks~1.2 and~2.3 switch to aluminium, building on our SSP-consistent dynamic MFA of aluminium alloys.\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} If the AI-assisted workflow underperforms, its measured error profile is itself a result, and classes are built conventionally. If plasma results are too immature, another of our emerging processes is used.

\textbf{Open infrastructure and its maintenance.} The Pillar~II infrastructure is a decentralized open protocol, not a central database. Interfaces, quality checks and reference implementations are open (versioned releases, continuous integration); parameter sets and instances may be held under other licences (e.g., licensed or confidential data), provided they pass the same checks. Outputs feed maintained tools (Premise, Brightway, Regioinvent, mescal), and ecoinvent-dependent results are regenerated by licensed users. Maintenance of the core is supported by the CIRAIG (International Reference Centre for Life Cycle Assessment and Sustainable Transition), whose industrial chairs have been continuously funded by partners for about 25 years.

\textbf{Collaborations and partners.} The program is conducted with partners of CIRAIG's International Research Consortium, notably ArcelorMittal (steel), Hydro-Québec (electricity) and OCP (phosphorus), who contribute data, help define the decision options compared in each case, and serve as early adopters of the resulting CLCA workflows. Joint research<+citation+> and ongoing collaboration with IIASA support the IAM work of Pillar~I, while process engineers and metallurgists of Polytechnique's chemical engineering department bring complementary expertise to Pillar~II.

\textbf{Relevance for equity and environmental justice.} Consequential LCA asks not only how large the consequences of a decision are, but where they fall: which mines, smelters or power plants respond, and therefore which communities bear the burdens.\ci{holland2016electricvehicles,owen2023landconnected} Generic datasets, often derived from European or North American contexts, average this distribution away. Our research makes it visible at three levels: marginal suppliers resolved to local markets and, where data allow, to plants (Tasks~1.2 and~1.3);\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025} technology variants resolved by age and emission controls, since a minority of old, poorly controlled units concentrates pollution where these fleets operate;\ci{tong2018superpolluting} and process models recontextualized beyond the high-income regions that dominate LCA data. Results will be reported by region and compared with public spatial data relevant in Canada (income, Indigenous lands, remote regions). They can inform analyses of distributional justice, but do not address procedural justice, which would require community partnerships we do not claim.\ci{jenkins2016energyjustice}

\textbf{Diversity of perspectives in model design.} Several modelling choices privilege some perspectives over others: whose futures the base scenarios represent, whose economic logic drives market responses, whose data inform process models, and whose values set rankings. We make these choices explicit rather than treating them as technical defaults. (i) Base scenarios embed assumptions about growth and inequality; we include low-demand pathways, which mainstream sets under-represent.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} (ii) Marginal-supplier methods assume that markets respond through least-cost investment, placing new capacity where production is cheapest, a choice with fairness implications;\ci{pachauri2022fairness} the framework records the assumptions behind each market response. (iii) Engineering literature and AI models are skewed towards high-income regions,\ci{manvi2024llmgeobias} so the evaluation of Task~2.2 includes process instances outside high-income countries where reference data exist, and recontextualized instances carry data-quality flags. (iv) Time horizons and weighting sets can reverse rankings, and preferred weights differ between income groups,\ci{deschryver2013valuechoices,bayazitsubasi2024weighting} so rankings are reported under alternative value sets. Each case study records these choices in a shared assumptions register reviewed with trainees.

\textbf{Significance.} We do not aim to resolve every difficulty of CLCA, but the core limitation that has kept it marginal: its results rest on representations of markets and technologies that are inconsistent with one another and with transition pathways, and whose errors are unknown. Lacking a trusted alternative, decision-makers routinely answer consequential questions with attributional tools, which can mislead them.\ci{Ekvall2004} By deriving market responses and technology representations from the same macro-scale scenarios, this program aims to make p-CLCA consistent and aligned with transition pathways, and to characterize, in contrasting cases, what common simplifications cost. Crucially, it delivers this as a workflow and an open infrastructure that others can adopt and extend: market responses tied to declared scenarios, and process-model classes that the community can instantiate, refine and connect, rather than rebuilding inventories study by study. Its reach would therefore extend well beyond our own cases: if successful, it would change what the LCA community can offer, answering consequential questions with consequential tools at the resolution of individual design, operation and deployment decisions. For Canadian decision-makers in metallurgy, fertilizer and electricity, it will provide a way to test whether such choices support the transition pathways they plan for. The program is interdisciplinary by construction, coupling industrial ecology (LCA, MFA), energy-economic modelling (IAMs, ESMs) and chemical and metallurgical process engineering, with data science as a common means; its HQP are trained at these interfaces.
