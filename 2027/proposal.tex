\noindent \textbf{Description of the Research Program}

\section{Context, research gap, and opportunity}

National energy and decarbonization policies, along with international climate agreements, are largely guided by large-scale energy and climate modelling tools such as Energy System Models (ESMs) and Integrated Assessment Models (IAMs).\ci{Pauliuk2017} The global IAM modelling community has converged on a manageable number of representative socioeconomic and climate mitigation pathways (Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) and Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs)) mapping different types of internally consistent potential futures.\ci{oneillNewScenarioFramework2014} Similarly, governments and industrial groups define roadmaps toward circular economy targets based on macro-scale material flow analyses (MFAs).\ci{aistRoadmapIronSteel2025,alonsoWorldMineralsOutlook,canadaplasticspactRoadmap2025Shared2021} These tools are designed to guide economy-wide decisions, such as national investment, taxation, and waste management policies. However, they lack the resolution to inform the everyday trade-offs made by industries and consumers. In other words, these macro-models have allowed for the exploration of useful global transition scenarios, but they are too coarse to offer specific guidance to the thousands of individual decisions that contribute to their implementation.

Such micro-scale environmental sustainability decisions are instead increasingly guided by a family of Attributional Life-Cycle Assessment (ALCA) tools, which estimate the share of environmental impacts that can be linked directly or indirectly to a specific product or function.\ci{Hellweg2014,hellwegLifecycleAssessmentGuide2023} Carbon footprints, environmental product declarations, and organizational LCAs are now broadly employed in product design, corporate reporting, and sustainable consumption guidance. In their common application, these assessments are mostly \emph{retrospective} analyses, relying on past observation data. In a rapidly transitioning economy, this reliance on near-present descriptions of processes and value chains limits the ability of decision makers to use ALCA results for forward-looking strategic decisions. Recently, prospective-ALCA (p-ALCA) databases such as PREMISE have begun incorporating macro-model scenario data into their process, market mix, and value chain descriptions.\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022}. These developments bring greater temporal consistency for the environmental sustainability assessment of long-lived products and future investments.

Major inconsistencies persist, however, between p-ALCAs at the micro-scale and transition models at the macro-scale. Energy systems illustrate the problem clearly. If an industry operates in a region already supplied by clean electricity, its p-ALCA results will show a limited contribution of electricity consumption to its footprint, and therefore limited benefits in further improving its energy efficiency. Yet, energy system models often identify efficiency gains in these same industries as essential for freeing clean electricity capacity to decarbonize other sectors. What appears negligible in a static footprinting assessment can thus be critical in the dynamic transition context. These contradictions arise because p-ALCAs remain \emph{attributional} in nature: they are limited to the direct value chains under study, and thus cannot capture market-mediated or system-wide consequences that shape the overall transition.\ci{Heijungs2001,Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons} Material cycles reveal a similar issue. A p-ALCA might, for instance, recommend maximizing the recycled content of a product, while dynamic material-flow analyses (dyn-MFAs) reveal that limited in-use stocks will constrain recycling supply for years.\ci{Pauliuk2015TakingStock} Increasing recycled content in one product may simply divert scarce secondary material from another, yielding little overall benefit.% These inconsistencies arise because all ALCAs are fundamentally unconstrained, linear models;\ci{Heijungs2001} they do not capture material or resource limits that are central to macro-scale transition strategies.

Capturing such bottlenecks and market-mediated mechanisms has long been the aim of so-called Consequential LCAs (CLCAs), which focus on quantifying the direct and indirect environmental \emph{consequences} of decisions (or changes) in production and consumption.\ci{Zamagni2012} In short, whereas ALCAs describe how impacts are distributed within the value chains of a given economy, CLCAs quantify how impacts change when the economy itself is perturbed by an individual decision.\ci{Ekvall2004}

Yet, despite being theorized already in the early 2000s,\ci{Ekvall2004} CLCAs remain a niche modelling approach.\ci{bamberComparingSourcesAnalysis2020} CLCAs require practitioners to identify which industries would respond to a change in the demand level for each product in the analysis (marginal providers).\ci{Weidema2003} This, of course, is highly uncertain and context-dependent.\ci{Mathiesen2009} Without a common framework, each practitioner was left to define \emph{ad hoc} scenarios (which are subjective and hard to compare), rely on very limited empirical data, or restrict their detailed modelling to a small part of the product system (the foreground), leading to inconsistencies with background databases representing the rest of the economy. So far, building CLCAs has proven time-consuming and expensive, with results that were often difficult to interpret and viewed with skepticism. Its results rest on simplifications whose errors are largely unknown. Furthermore, CLCAs are almost invariably defined relative to one system scenario: assuming that the \emph{status quo} prevails over the duration the decision under study.\ci{Steubing2016} This static assumption is ill-suited for major investment and production decisions in a rapidly evolving economy.

We see a \textbf{strategic opportunity} to build prospective-CLCAs (p-CLCAs) as a systematic bridge between macro-ESM/IAM/MFA models and micro-decisions, anchoring these analyses as perturbations of representative scenarios and ensuring that LCA guidance aligns with broader transition pathways.


\section{Core challenges and objectives}

We focus on two core challenges of CLCA that are addressed with an overarching integration strategy.

The first challenge (\textbf{Pillar I)} relates to the representation of markets, and how they respond to marginal decisions in consumption levels. This would ideally be captured through sophisticated economic models, but this is completely impractical; the thousands of markets in a CLCA are instead represented by simplifying heuristics. The problem is that these heuristics are currently (a) not constrained/contained by a consistent narrative, (b) wildly different in their level of sophistication, without characterization of their associated errors, and (c) inferred from trends rather than from the mechanisms that drive capacity investment (e.g., the timing of demand relative to peak in electricity systems). Our objective (\textbf{SO-1}) is therefore to develop a scenario-consistent framework for the identification of marginal suppliers, in which the responses of all markets in an assessment are derived from the same base scenario, whether by perturbing a model or by applying heuristics to its outputs, informed by these mechanisms, with a characterization of the errors associated with different levels of simplifications and assumptions.

The second challenge (\textbf{Pillar II}) relates to the representation of technological processes, and how they are influenced by operation decisions and deployment context. These would ideally be captured through sophisticated engineering models, but this is completely impractical; currently, the thousands of processes in CLCA are represented with very limited parametrization or, most often, completely fixed black-box models. Prospective databases make these black boxes evolve along macro-scenarios by rescaling selected inputs and emissions,\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} which aligns them with the scenario but not necessarily with the physics of the process. When highly parametrized engineering models are used in LCA, these are not formatted in a way that allows for their reuse in other studies or their integration with other process models. The problem is that these process simplifications and incompatibilities (a) prevent our assessment of process design and operational decisions in CLCA and (b) obscure real differences between variations of a technology depending on its location, context, age, etc. There are limitted benefits to correctly identifying the marginal provider of a market (Pillar~I) if all suppliers are assumed to have the same technological recipe regardless of context and geography. This rigid approach also (c) constitutes a major source of inefficiency in LCA work, limitting our community's ability to collaborate on common models and reuse them in different contexts. Our objective (\textbf{SO-2}) is therefore twofold: to create an interoperability framework capable of containing process models of all levels of sophistication in an interoperable manner, representing specific technologies as instances of a broader, coherent \emph{process-model classes}, instantiated from the base scenario for the context and technology evolution they face (grid mix, feedstock availability, vintage, efficiency frontier), and from the decision under study for their design and operation; and to pioneer an efficient workflow to populate such process-model classes through the generation of reduced engineering models in situations with differing levels of data richness (established processes, emerging technologies, etc.)

These objectives are distilled in three research questions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{RQ-I.} How can the response of markets to a decision be derived consistently, from models of different types or from market evidence, down to the local markets where decisions play out, and what errors do common shortcuts introduce?
	\item \textbf{RQ-II.} How can technologies be represented so that what their variants share is modelled once, while the consequences of differences in context, vintage, operation and maturity are captured; and how can such models be built and reduced efficiently?
	\item \textbf{Program-level question.} How much do these representation choices change the conclusions of p-CLCA, across types of decisions?
\end{itemize}

In both pillars, our \textbf{common strategy} is to (a) mirror each conceptual framework in open-source software, in support of desirable change in community practice: an integration framework for market responses, implemented as an open layer on existing tools (e.g., premise, Brightway) in Pillar~I, and a modelling framework for process-model classes, implemented as a new open digital infrastructure in Pillar~II; (b) resolve what macro-scenarios aggregate, broad regions and technology classes, by representing the high-resolution interactions needed in LCA as specific instances of broader markets or of more general classes of processes, all derived from the same base scenario; (c) accommodate a wide range of simplifying assumptions in a consistent manner to a fit differing levels of data availability and resolution, but (d) contribute to testing these simplifying heuristics against more sophisticated models to gain insights in their associated uncertainties and biases. We will explore these through a range of environmental CLCA case studies with differing levels of data availability for both market and process causation, each evaluated against at least two contrasting SSP/RCP base scenarios, including a low-demand pathway.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025}

These specific objectives contribute to our \textbf{long-term goal} of transforming how p-CLCA is performed and aligning it, in both market responses and technology evolution, with macro-scale transition pathway scenarios. We aim for useful, pragmatic and discriminating environmental decision support in various consumption and process design contexts. % Our strategic positionning is that this is achievable if we maximize the use (and reuse) of available data and models through a smart integration and a flexible, open modelling framework; consolidation of these models as disaggregations and perturbations of macro-scenarios; and a systematic, quantitative approach to simplifying assumptions in the representation of economic and engineering causal mechanisms.

%\textbf{Long-term goal.} Our long-term goal is a consequential LCA that can tell decision-makers how a specific choice of technology, design, operation or deployment changes environmental flows in a world in transition, and how far that answer can be trusted. This requires representations of markets and technologies that are general enough to cover the economy, specific enough to respond to the decisions and contexts that matter, consistent with transition scenarios, and tested so that their errors are known. Beyond this grant, we aim to contribute to an open, community-maintained ecosystem of such representations. We do not compete with economists or process engineers on their own models: we build reductions of these models suited to consequential LCA, connect them consistently, and measure what each simplification costs in terms of decisions.

\underline{Recent progress:} Our recent work provides the starting points for such a p-CLCA integrated development. For market responses, we developed guidelines and a timing-shift method for deriving marginal suppliers from IAM scenarios,\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} calibrated the open-source ESM EnergyScope-Quebec, and integrated LCA metrics in ESM optimization.\ci{souttreMescalToolCoupling2025} For databases, we regionalized ecoinvent with trade data\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} and pushed forward its reconciliation with mass conservation.\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} For material flows, we built SSP-consistent aluminium scenarios,\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} quantified metal bottlenecks along IAM energy transitions scenarios,\ci{bieuvilleMetalBottlenecksEnergy2025} and assembled site-level databases on Canadian metal production.\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025,pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} For processes, we developed highly-parametrized waste management process models\ci{ravoahangyParametrizationBiowasteComposting2026,tanguay-riouxCanHydrothermalCarbonization2026} A prototype infrastructure for process models (v0) is being implemented with software-development support from the Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI).

%\underline{Market responses are inferred from trends rather than from the mechanisms that produce them.} How a market responds to a change in demand depends on its state (growing, in overcapacity, constrained by resources or scrap), on the size and timing of the change and, for electricity, on when demand occurs. Practice compresses this response into rules applied to market trends: in a growing market, the technologies whose production grows are assumed to supply additional demand.\ci{Weidema2003} Consequential databases apply such rules to projected growth, because running energy-system models consistently for every market is impractical.\ci{vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} Applied to IAM outputs, they give results that are highly sensitive to the time interval chosen and differ substantially between models.\ci{maesProspectiveConsequentialLife2023} Moreover, growth does not show a response to demand: a technology may grow because of mandates or falling costs that would occur regardless of the decision. Energy research derives long-run marginal emission factors from models of capacity expansion,\ci{hawkesLongrunMarginalCO22014,gagnonPlanningEvolutionElectric2022} and early work showed that such analyses and simple rules can point to different marginal technologies.\ci{Mathiesen2009} But this practice is largely limited to electricity, the errors of the rules LCA uses instead have not been measured systematically, and for materials, open models that can be perturbed are scarce.

%% \underline{Technology representations are either generic or built case by case.} A useful representation must capture what the variants of a technology share, so that models are efficient to build and reuse, and how they differ in context, vintage, operating conditions, feedstock and maturity, since consequential questions often turn on these differences: whether an idle old plant or a new one responds, how a process is designed or operated, what an emerging process will look like once deployed.\ci{Majeau-Bettez2021} LCA databases offer breadth without this specificity. Each dataset describes one technology in one setting, is often transferred to other regions with few changes, and rarely distinguishes vintages or operating conditions. Regionalized databases, including our own Regioinvent, adapt the electricity, heat, waste treatment and supply markets a process draws on to its country, not the technology itself.\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} Site-level data show what this hides: across Canadian mining sites producing the same product, our assessment found impacts differing by up to a factor of 13, and by up to a factor of 6 from the proxy datasets normally used.\ci{pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} Parametrized and engineering process models offer specificity without breadth: each is built for one study or sector, with its own conventions, and is rarely designed to connect to others.

%\section{Shared protocol: scenarios, perturbations and tests of validity}

%\textbf{Base scenarios.} Each decision is evaluated against several base scenarios: SSP/RCP pathways from published IAM ensembles,\ci{riahiSharedSocioeconomicPathways2017} completed for metals with stock-and-flow trajectories consistent with the same socioeconomic assumptions (O1.2). The set includes low-demand and sufficiency pathways, which mainstream scenario sets under-represent.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} Scenarios set the market conditions of Pillar I, the boundary conditions of the regional energy model (import and carbon prices) and the context of process models in Pillar II (grid mix, feedstock availability). Results are reported with their robustness across scenarios.

%\textbf{Perturbations.} A decision is represented as a perturbation: a change in demand, design or operation, defined by its size, timing and profile and by whether suppliers can anticipate it, in the same way in every model. Changes in how a process is designed or operated (Pillar II) reach markets (Pillar I) through what they change in demand, such as a load profile or a co-product output.

%\textbf{Tests of validity.} The consequences of a decision cannot be observed directly: they are the difference between two futures, only one of which happens.\ci{oreskesVerificationValidationConfirmation1994} Validity is therefore tested in three ways. Internal consistency is checked in code on every model update: mass and energy balances, which our reconciliation of ecoinvent showed are far from guaranteed in current databases,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} supply equal to demand in each market, and production within capacity. Each simplified representation is compared with a more complete model where one can be run, and with observations where they exist (capacity actually built, plant statistics). Where neither exists, as for undeployed technologies, confidence rests on the errors measured in the first two settings. Both pillars use the same two success criteria: (i) agreement with the reference on the quantities that drive results, measured as the relative error on the impact intensity of the marginal supply mix (Pillar I) or of the main process flows (Pillar II); and (ii) preservation of decision rankings, measured as the rate of pairwise rank reversals across decision alternatives and impact categories. A small deviation that reverses a ranking counts as a failure. Tolerances are set for each case in the protocol, before results are known. The result is an error profile in the settings tested, not a universal threshold.

\section{Pillar I: market consequences}

%\textbf{Concept.} A market consequence is usually summarized by a marginal supplier or mix. We separate it into three questions that can each be tested: how much of a change in demand becomes a change in production, once prices and binding limits such as emission caps adjust; how much of that production comes from new rather than existing capacity, a share that changes over time; and which technologies and suppliers provide it. Current practice addresses mainly the third, assuming that extra demand is met in full by new capacity.\ci{vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} The output is a layer, built on existing open tools (pyam, premise, Brightway), that provides LCA databases with marginal supply mixes per market, documenting for each the scenario and the kind of evidence behind it. Artificial intelligence (AI) serves as a coding aid: it reduces the effort of scripting large batches of model runs and of building audited pipelines for market data, with every value traced to its source.

%\textbf{Approach.} The framework of SO-1 derives the marginal suppliers of every market from the same base scenario, so that all market responses in an LCA follow one transition narrative. Base scenarios are published SSP/RCP pathways,\ci{riahiSharedSocioeconomicPathways2017} including low-demand ones,\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} completed for materials, where IAMs are thin, by trajectories consistent with the same socioeconomic drivers (Task~1.2). Given the state of each market in the scenario (growth or decline, unused capacity), short-term responses are drawn from unused capacity, and long-term responses from changes in the timing of the scenario's capacity additions and retirements, building on our timing-shift method.\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} Because no single method suits all markets, the framework accepts methods of increasing simplification, and records which one was used for each market: perturbation of a structural model where one exists, heuristics applied to published scenario outputs, and market evidence (installed capacity, utilization, announced projects) where neither exists. The tasks measure what each simplification costs on two criteria shared with Pillar~II: agreement with the reference on the impact intensity of the marginal supply, and preservation of the ranking of decision alternatives, with tolerances set for each case before results are known. Tasks~1.1 to~1.3 test the framework against a structural model, then against observations, then extend it where no full reference exists (rows of Figure~1). The framework is released as open software built on existing tools (pyam, premise, Brightway).

\textbf{Task 1.1. Marginal suppliers tested against structural energy models} \emph{(SO-1; PhD~I, years 1--4).} \emph{How large are the errors of CLCA marginal-supplier heuristics relative to the response of a structural model, and on what do they depend?} %
%
We will start from at least one global IAM model (likely open-source GCAM, and MESSAGE through ongoing collaboration) and a regionalized dynamic ESM (EnergyScope-Quebec) and will apply marginal perturbations in the energy sector. This will enable us to investigate how the transition pathways are affected, and how much the consequences of a perturbation on a common scenario depend on model-type being perturbed. This variability will inform a base level of uncertainty. It will also help identify to what extent the response to a perturbation translates in (a) changing capacity utilization of existing infrastructure (short-erm consequential effects), changes in demand by other actors (demand elasticity), or new capacity installation (so-called long-term consequential effects). Perturbations will also differ in their load profile (e.g., winter-peaking heating versus flat industrial demand), to test whether the timing of demand, and not only its annual volume, determines the capacity that responds; EnergyScope-Quebec's peak-hour resolution captures this mechanism, which global IAMs represent only coarsely.

Of course, it is not generally feasible to directly perturb macro-models, and we will compare the above results to different heuristics that are applicable to different levels of data availability. For example, what errors are introduced by our representation of perturbations as shifting the timing of planned investments in the transition scenario, compared to a full model run? How do these compare with heuristics based on trends in installed capacities, or trends in production volume?

\textbf{Task 1.2. Material markets: hindcast and constrained secondary supply} \emph{(SO-1; MSc~I, years 2--3).} \emph{Would heuristics have identified the metal production capacity actually built, and when does extra demand for recycled metal fall on primary production? }
%
Contrary to energy systems, metal markets lack open economic models that can be readily perturbed, but their past capacity additions can be observed. Looking backward, we apply CLCA heuristics to the information available at each date between 2000 and 2020 and compares the suppliers they identify with the capacity actually built, using plant trackers and company filings. This offers an evaluation of the heuristics employed in CLCAs, their applicability, and their limitations. Then, looking forward, we perturb a stock-driven material flow model under two or three SSPs, yielding estimates of prospective marginal consequential supply mixes for CLCAs. Of particular interest will be a capacity driver specific to metals, secondary supply constrained by in-use stocks and alloy quality: how sorting rates and efforts to avoid interalloy contamination ---a priority in circular economy--- affect the evolution of the marginal mix and pCLCA performances. These studies will be applied to iron and steel (through long-standing partnership with ArcelorMittal in the CIRAIG Consortium) and aluminium, building on our SSP-consistent dynamic aluminium alloy MFA model.\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} \emph{Outcome:} a historical test of marginal-supplier heuristics, and scenario-consistent trajectories of secondary metal supply for the framework.

\textbf{Task 1.3. From regional scenarios to local markets} \emph{(SO-1; MSc~III, years 4--5).} \emph{Does representing local markets as instances of regional scenario markets change the marginal suppliers enough to change decisions? }
%
 IAM scenarios describe large world regions and broad technology classes, and a growing regional trend can hide declines in specific markets, limitting their applicability in p-CLCAs. We will test different disaggregation algorithms (going beyond \textcite{sferraDSCALEV01Opensource2026}) to investigate their influence on regionalized p-CLCAs. We will leverage local land-use data, demographic trends, plant capacities, trade flows and national plans, and compares the resulting marginal suppliers and impacts with those of the regional scenario and of the global-model perturbations of Task~1.1. Our case study will focus on fertilizer (nitrogen and phosphorus) flows forecasted by global MFAs and IAMs (notably GLOBIOM), markets in which resource concentration (phosphate rock) and trade shape which supply capacity responds, leveraging our collaboration with OCP through the CIRAIG's Consortium. \emph{Outcome:} a tested method to derive local marginal suppliers from regional scenarios, and an estimation of its sensitivity to diaggregation assumptions.

\textbf{Task 2.1. An interoperability framework for process models} \emph{(SO-2; PhD~II, years 1--4).} \emph{What must a process-model interface expose for models of any sophistication to be exchanged and connected without loss of consistency, and where in current databases does it matter most? }
%
The thesis starts with an assessment of the level of sophistication and quality control in the parametrization of processes of a centralized CLCA database (consquential ecoinvent): the share of processes with (a) 100\% yields, (b) fundamental balances that fail to close,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026}, (c) a single technology identical across regions and vintages, etc. A similar assessment will be applied to reduced engineering models employed in LCAs, complemented by an analysis of their openness and reusability in other studies. We hypothesize that this study will confirm a genuine tradeoff between high sophistication and flexibility in individual studies and high reusability in centralized databases.

The thesis then proposes a digital framework to support a \emph{decentralized} approach to the publication of interoperable process classes capable of containing the full range of reduced process models. This approach will build upon and generalize a preliminary (v0) digital infrastructure developed for 2PURES FCI project on plastic circularity. The core design strategy is to wrap technology models of all complexities ---from near black-box processes, XGBOOST, phenomenological models <+reformulate+>--- in a compatibility layer that (a) enforces tests of physical plausibility, (b) separates the parameters set by the base scenario (context, vintage, efficiency frontier) from those set by the decision under study (design, operation), so that instances are derived consistently from macro-scenarios, and (c) exposes the necessary flows information to connect these instances within p-CLCA system representation. A central question concerns co-products, a long-standing focus of our group.\ci{Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons,provost-savardSubstitutionModelingCan2024} When operating conditions change the yields and qualities of co-products (for instance, the hydrochar and process water of hydrothermal carbonization, used as fuel, soil amendment or biogas feedstock), the substitutions they induce change too, and the interface must let the consequential treatment follow the operating state.

We will then test this infrastructure by packaging the different parametrized models found in the litterature, deriving specific instances of each process to match different geographical and technology vintage contexts, and connect these instances together and within the flows of a broader CLCA database. We will mesure the level of work necessary for this data specification and integration, and quantify the influence of this disaggregation on consequential scores.  \emph{Outcome:} a general-purpose process development and packaging interface, with documented design principles, tested for broad applicability with published models from the litterature and through Tasks~2.2 and~2.3.

\textbf{Task 2.2. An efficient workflow, from documented to emerging processes} \emph{(SO-2; PhD~III, years 2--5).} \emph{Can a human-in-the-loop workflow build functional reduced models from publicly accessible material with much less effort, and with what errors? }%
%
The strategy behind the infrastructure from Task~2.1 is that it proves more efficient on the long-run to invest in a common, flexible, and quality controled technology model that can be instantiated to reflect different contexts rather than separately inventorying variants of this same technology in different geographies and vintages. Nevertheless, in the short term, the initial development of reduced engineering models constitutes a major investment. 
In Task~2.2, we investigate to what extent the data collection and process modelling and coding can be automated through the use of generative artificial intelligence, and to what extent we can ensure the documentation and transparency of the resulting code, notably by relying a human-in-the-loop and the buit-in quality controls of the digital infrastructure of Task~2.1. In this workflow, gen-AI drafts model code from first principles and published material, and people review it; every parameter is traced to a cited source, and all checks run as ordinary code. In years 2--3, the workflow is evaluated against reference models (our composting, biomethanization and hydrothermal carbonization models, and at least one model with little public documentation, to bound the advantage of well-documented processes), comparing structure, balance closure, outputs and effort. In years 3--4, it addresses a major uncertainty of low-maturity processes, the flowsheet itself, by assembling alternative configurations from reusable unit-operation modules and design heuristics,\ci{douglasHierarchicalDecisionProcedure1985} with maturity expressed through physical parameters (yields, specific energy) rather than generic learning rates, after checking that it recovers the configuration of a known process. In years 4--5, it is applied to plasma nitrogen fixation as a potential alternative to both fungicide treatment and conventional fertilizers from Haber-Bosch ammonia, building on ongoing <+PLANET research+>; its alternative configurations are evaluated under the electricity and fertilizer-demand contexts of the base scenarios (Tasks~1.1 and~1.3). Jointly with Task~1.3, it asks whether uncertainty about the deployed configuration of an emerging process exceeds uncertainty about the suppliers that respond to its demand. \emph{Outcome:} an evaluated workflow with its error profile, and a configuration-resolved model of an emerging process.

\textbf{Task 2.3. Reduced models of established, data-rich processes: metallurgy} \emph{(SO-2; MSc~II, years 2--3).} \emph{How far do engineering models of an established process deviate from reported plant performance, and does a calibrated, vintage-resolved model change consequential results relative to generic datasets?} %
%
First-principle reduced models are a useful basis to quickly grow a constitent base of reusable process models, or to prospectively explore the potential of novel technologies (Task~2.2), but there remains a risk that it may fail to capture real-world inefficiencies, requirements and emissions. Task~2.3 reality-tests this approach against well-documented process observations. It will develop a steel class resolved by vintage for one or two routes (blast furnace--basic oxygen furnace; direct reduction--electric arc furnace with natural gas, then hydrogen) from published engineering relations. In partnership with ArcelorMittal, it then develops the calibration step of the workflow of Task~2.2: estimating, from plant and sector statistics, the inefficiencies that design models leave out (part load, start-ups, losses). The calibrated class is then instantiated along scenario trajectories (scrap availability from Task~1.2, vintage structure of installed capacity), and the efficiency improvements that IAM scenarios assume for the sector are checked against its physical limits. It compares the calibrated class both with its prior and with ecoinvent datasets for internal consistency and for its effect on consequential results. \emph{Outcome:} a calibrated, reusable steel class, a measure of what generic datasets miss, and a physical consistency check of scenario assumptions for steel.

\section{Integration, plan and risks}

Integration does not wait for the end of the program. The common strategy and a cumulative table of case results are set up in year 1, maintained by PhD I and PhD II. Energy is a Pillar I case; metallurgy and nitrogen are studied by both pillars at the same time (centre of Figure 1). On metallurgy (years 2--3, with ArcelorMittal), Tasks~1.2 and~2.3 test whether distinguishing idle, often old, capacity from new capacity close to best available technology changes which supplier responds and with what flows. On nitrogen (years 4--5), Tasks~1.3 and~2.2 ask which matters more for an emerging process: uncertainty about its deployed configuration, or about the suppliers that respond to its demand.

\textbf{Risks and fallbacks.} Each objective yields publishable results on its own. If the global model proves too costly to operate, Task~1.1 rests on EnergyScope Québec and published scenario outputs. If steel data are inaccessible, Tasks~1.2 and~2.3 switch to aluminium. \section{Open infrastructure, partners, equity and significance}

\textbf{Open infrastructure and its maintenance.} The infrastructure is an open protocol rather than a new central database. Class interfaces, quality checks and reference implementations are open (versioned releases, continuous integration); parameter sets and instances may be held under different licences (licensed databases, confidential company data exported as aggregated inventories), provided they pass the same checks. Results that depend on ecoinvent are regenerated by licensed users, and outputs feed tools that are already maintained (premise, Brightway, Regioinvent, mescal). Long-term maintenance of the core is supported through the CIRAIG (International Reference Centre for Life Cycle Assessment and Sustainable Transition), whose industrial research chairs have been funded continuously by partners for about 25 years.

\textbf{Partners.} ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec and Québec's Ministère de l'Économie, de l'Innovation et de l'Énergie are full CIRAIG partners, contributing funding, data and engagement. In the metallurgy and energy cases, they help define the decision alternatives to compare, so that the ranking criterion is tested on decisions that matter in practice.

\textbf{Equity, diversity and inclusion in the research.} Which suppliers respond to a decision determines where its burdens fall.\ci{holland2016electricvehicles,owen2023landconnected} Because the program resolves consequences to countries and, where data allow, to individual plants,\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025} results can be reported by region and compared with public spatial data on characteristics relevant in Canada (income, Indigenous lands, remote regions). This addresses the distribution of burdens, not procedural justice, which would require community partnerships we do not claim. Value choices also matter: time horizons and weighting sets can reverse rankings, and preferred weights differ between income groups,\ci{deschryver2013valuechoices,bayazitsubasi2024weighting} so rankings are reported under alternative value sets. Because AI models and the engineering literature are skewed towards high-income regions,\ci{manvi2024llmgeobias} the evaluation of Task~2.2 includes process instances outside high-income countries where reference data exist.

\textbf{Significance.} For LCA, the program will provide consequential results consistent with macro-scale transition scenarios in both market responses and technology evolution, with known errors, and evidence on when more detail changes conclusions. For Canadian decision-makers in metallurgy, fertilizer and electricity, it will provide a way to test whether a design, operating or deployment choice supports the transition pathways they plan for. Its HQP will combine LCA, system models, process modelling and verified data pipelines.
