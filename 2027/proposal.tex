\noindent \textbf{Description of the Research Program}

\section{Context, research gap, and opportunity}

National decarbonization policies and international climate agreements are largely guided by energy system models (ESMs) and integrated assessment models (IAMs),\ci{Pauliuk2017} whose community has converged on a small set of internally consistent socioeconomic and mitigation narratives: the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) and Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs).\ci{oneillNewScenarioFramework2014} Similarly, circular-economy roadmaps rest on macro-scale material flow analyses (MFAs).\ci{aistRoadmapIronSteel2025,alonsoWorldMineralsOutlook,canadaplasticspactRoadmap2025Shared2021} These tools guide economy-wide policy, but are too coarse to inform the thousands of isolated decisions by consumers and firms that each shape these transitions.

These micro- and meso-scale decisions are instead increasingly guided by attributional life-cycle assessment (ALCA), which estimates the share of environmental impacts linked to a product or function, as in carbon footprints and environmental product declarations.\ci{Hellweg2014,hellwegLifecycleAssessmentGuide2023} Most ALCAs are \emph{retrospective}, relying on past data, which limits their use for forward-looking decisions. Prospective ALCA (p-ALCA) databases such as Premise now incorporate IAM macro-scenario data,\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} bringing temporal consistency to the assessment of long-lived products and investments. Major inconsistencies persist, however, between micro-scale p-ALCAs and macro-scale transition models. An industry already supplied by clean electricity will see, in its p-ALCA, little benefit in further improving its energy efficiency; yet ESMs often deem such gains essential to free clean electricity capacity for other sectors. Material cycles show the same problem: a p-ALCA may recommend maximizing the recycled content of a product, while dynamic MFAs show that limited in-use stocks will constrain scrap supply for years,\ci{Pauliuk2015TakingStock} so that more recycled content in one product may simply divert scarce secondary material from another. Both contradictions arise because p-ALCAs remain \emph{attributional}: limited to the value chains under study, they cannot capture the market-mediated, system-wide consequences that shape the transition.\ci{Heijungs2001,Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons}

Capturing such mechanisms has long been the aim of consequential LCA (CLCA):\ci{Zamagni2012} whereas ALCAs describe how impacts are distributed within the value chains of a given economy, CLCAs quantify how impacts change when a decision perturbs the economy, its \emph{consequences}.\ci{Ekvall2004} Yet, though theorized in the early 2000s, CLCA remains a niche approach.\ci{bamberComparingSourcesAnalysis2020} It requires identifying which suppliers would respond to a change in demand for each product (marginal suppliers),\ci{Weidema2003} which is highly uncertain and context-dependent.\ci{Mathiesen2009} Guidelines provide simplified rules for this identification, and consequential databases have attempted to apply them across the economy,\ci{Weidema2003,vandepaerIntegrationLongtermMarginal2019} but with heterogeneous assumptions and little internal consistency. Extensions of these rules for prospective analyses are only recent and divergent.\ci{maesProspectiveConsequentialLife2023,provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} These rules rely on market trends and scarce empirical data, and detailed modelling is often restricted to a few processes in the foreground of the product system, inconsistently with the background database. Building CLCAs has thus proven costly, and their results, resting on simplifications whose errors are largely unknown, are often hard to interpret and viewed with skepticism. Finally, CLCAs are almost always defined against a single scenario, the \emph{status quo} over the lifetime of the decision,\ci{Steubing2016} an assumption ill-suited to robust investment and production decisions in a transitioning economy.

We see a \textbf{strategic opportunity} to make prospective CLCA (p-CLCA) a systematic bridge between macro-scale models (ESMs, IAMs, MFAs) and micro- and meso-scale decisions, anchoring each analysis as a  perturbation of broader representative scenarios: this aligns CLCA guidance with transition pathways and bounds its many degrees of freedom by a shared narrative.

\section{Core challenges and objectives}

We address two core challenges of CLCA through a common strategy. The first (\textbf{Pillar I}) is to represent how markets respond to marginal changes in demand. Sophisticated economic models would ideally capture this, but cannot be run for the thousands of markets in a CLCA, which are instead represented by simplifying heuristics. These heuristics are currently (a) not bound by a consistent narrative, (b) of widely varying sophistication, with uncharacterized errors, and (c) inferred from trends rather than from the mechanisms that drive capacity investment (e.g., the timing of demand relative to peak periods in electricity systems). Our objective (\textbf{O-1}) is therefore to develop and test an open-source framework for estimating market responses for CLCA consistently with a common transition scenario, across markets with differing levels of modelling and data richness (from perturbing IAMs and ESMs, to mechanistic simplifications, to coarse heuristics), and to characterize the errors of each simplification.

The second challenge (\textbf{Pillar II}) is to represent how technological processes respond to design and operating decisions and to deployment context. Sophisticated engineering models would ideally capture this, but cannot be built for the thousands of processes in a CLCA, which are instead represented with little parametrization or as fixed black boxes; prospective databases make these evolve along macro-scenarios by rescaling inputs and emissions, aligning them with the scenario but not necessarily with process physics.\ci{sacchiPRospectiveEnvironMentalImpact2022} These simplifications (a) prevent the assessment of design and operating decisions and (b) obscure differences between technology variants by location, context and age. Identifying the right marginal supplier (Pillar~I) is of little use if all suppliers share the same recipe. These simplifications also (c) make LCA work inefficient: detailed engineering models, where used in LCA, are rarely formatted for reuse or interconnection, limiting collaboration on common models and their adaptation to new contexts. Our objective (\textbf{O-2}) is therefore twofold. First, to develop and test an open interoperability framework that accommodates process models of any sophistication and represents technologies as instances of broader \emph{process-model classes}, parametrized by the base scenario for their context and technology evolution, and by the decision under study for their design and operation. Second, to develop an efficient workflow to populate these classes with reduced engineering models, from established to emerging processes, and to characterize its errors.

In both pillars, our \textbf{common strategy} is to (a) mirror each conceptual framework in open-source software, to support a change in community practice: an integration framework for market responses, as an open layer on existing tools (Pillar~I), and a modelling framework for process-model classes, as a new open digital infrastructure (Pillar~II); (b) resolve what macro-scenarios aggregate---broad regions and technology classes---by representing the markets and processes needed in CLCA as instances of broader ones, all anchored in the same base scenario; (c) accommodate simplifying assumptions consistently, to fit differing data availability and resolution; and (d) evaluate these simplifications against the strongest reference available (rows of the figure): their errors relative to more detailed models (Tasks~1.1, 1.2, 2.1 and~2.2) or to observed and reported data (built capacity, Task~1.2; plant performance, Task~2.3), and, where no such reference exists, the sensitivity of rankings to alternative representations (Task~1.3; emerging processes, Task~2.2). We pursue these through case studies, each evaluated under at least two contrasting SSP/RCP base scenarios, including a low-demand pathway.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025} Across cases, we measure what each simplification costs in decision terms: a representation choice is deemed decisive when it reverses the ranking of two options in a decision by their potential environmental consequences, beyond result uncertainty, on climate change or another indicator declared for the case, under at least one base scenario.

These short-term objectives contribute to our \textbf{long-term goal}: a p-CLCA practice built on shared, open representations of markets and technologies, maintained and extended by the community, that provides pragmatic, discriminating decision support for consumption, process design and operation in a world in transition.

\noindent \textbf{Recent progress.} For market responses, we developed guidelines and a timing-shift method for deriving marginal suppliers from IAM scenarios,\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} calibrated the open-source ESM EnergyScope-Quebec, and integrated LCA metrics in ESM optimization.\ci{souttreMescalToolCoupling2025} For databases, we regionalized ecoinvent with trade data\ci{agezRegioinventRegionalizedVersion2026} and advanced its reconciliation with mass balances.\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} For material flows, we built SSP-consistent aluminium scenarios,\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022} quantified metal bottlenecks along IAM energy-transition scenarios,\ci{bieuvilleMetalBottlenecksEnergy2025} and assembled site-level databases on Canadian metal production.\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025,pellanSiteSpecificLifeCycleAssessment2026} For processes, we developed highly parametrized waste management processes.\ci{ravoahangyParametrizationBiowasteComposting2026,tanguay-riouxCanHydrothermalCarbonization2026} A prototype infrastructure for process models (v0) is being built for plastic circularity, with software-development support from the Canada Foundation for Innovation (CFI, 2PURES project).

\section{Pillar I: market consequences}

\noindent \textbf{Task 1.1. Marginal suppliers tested against structural energy models} \emph{(O-1; PhD~I, years 1--4).} \emph{How large are the errors of CLCA marginal-supplier heuristics relative to a structural model's response, and on what do they depend?} %
%
The thesis first formalizes O-1 as a protocol and implements it in open-source software that captures how macro-models respond to perturbations, benchmarks simplifying heuristics against these responses, and yields scenario-tagged marginal supply mixes with their assumptions recorded. We then perturb energy demand, from marginal to sector scale, under contrasting base scenarios, in a global IAM (MESSAGE-GLOBIOM, with IIASA) and in a provincial dynamic ESM (EnergyScope-Quebec). We decompose each response into changes in the utilization of existing capacity (short term), in other actors' demand (elasticity, captured by the IAMs), and in new capacity (long term). In EnergyScope-Quebec, perturbations of equal annual volume but different load profiles (e.g., winter-peaking heating versus flat industrial demand) test whether, and by how much, the timing of demand changes the capacity that responds. As macro-models cannot be perturbed in every CLCA, we then benchmark heuristics applied to each model's baseline at different levels of data availability: our timing-shift method,\ci{provost-savardGuidelinesProspectiveConsequential2025} which brings forward the scenario's planned investments, and simpler trend-based heuristics (capacity, production). Errors are measured on the marginal mix (technology shares), on the per-unit impacts of marginal supply, and on rank reversals in the case decisions.

\noindent \textbf{Task 1.2. Metal markets: hindcast and secondary supply} \emph{(O-1; MSc~I, years 2--3).} \emph{Would heuristics have identified the steel production capacity actually built, and how do recycling dynamics shape marginal suppliers and end-of-life substitution?}
%
Metal markets lack open economic models that can readily be perturbed, so each question uses the best available evidence. Looking backward, we apply CLCA heuristics to the information available at each date between 2000 and 2020 and compare the suppliers they identify, by vintage, with the capacity actually built (plant trackers, company filings). Looking forward, we perturb our SSP-aligned dynamic MFA of aluminium alloys and their sorting\ci{pedneaultSectorspecificScenariosFuture2022,pedneaultHowMuchSorting2023a} to derive the market mechanisms of CLCA (marginal suppliers, substitution at end of life), so that they reflect scenario dynamics and the evolution of sorting rates and scrap quality, and compare them with the conventional assumption of constrained secondary supply. \emph{Outcome:} a historical falsification test of marginal-supplier heuristics and their domain of applicability, and scenario-consistent marginal suppliers and end-of-life substitution for a recycled metal.

\noindent \textbf{Task 1.3. From scenario regions to local markets} \emph{(O-1; MSc~III, years 4--5).} \emph{Does resolving local markets within scenario regions change marginal suppliers enough to change decisions?}
%
IAM scenarios describe large world regions and broad technology evolutions, but this aggregation can hide heterogeneous local starting points and trajectories. We first apply the framework of Task~1.1 to fertilizer (nitrogen and phosphorus) markets, as forecast by global MFAs and IAMs (notably GLOBIOM), to derive the evolution of their marginal suppliers under contrasting scenarios, at their native regional resolution. In these markets, resource concentration (phosphate rock) and trade shape which supply capacity responds; the study builds on our collaboration with OCP. We then test disaggregation algorithms (going beyond \textcite{sferraDSCALEV01Opensource2026}) to derive the evolution of local marginal suppliers, including in Canada, from demand-side (land use, demography) and supply-side data (mine and plant capacities, trade, national plans), and compare them with results at native resolution. \emph{Outcome:} a tested method to derive the evolution of local marginal suppliers from global scenarios, and its sensitivity to disaggregation assumptions.

\section{Pillar II: process consequences}

\noindent \textbf{Task 2.1. An interoperability framework to consolidate process knowledge for CLCA} \emph{(O-2; PhD~II, years 1--4).} \emph{What must a process-model interface expose for models of any sophistication to connect without loss of consistency, and how far do current databases and published models fall short of it?}
%
The thesis first assesses the sophistication and quality control of process parametrization in a centralized CLCA database (consequential ecoinvent): the share of processes with (a) idealized 100\% yields, (b) mass or energy balances that fail to close,\ci{dewachterMassbalancedEcoinventLife2026} or (c) one technology identical across regions and vintages. We then systematically review reduced engineering models published for LCA across sectors and modelling paradigms, characterizing their openness and reusability; this corpus defines the breadth of models the interface must accommodate. We hypothesize a genuine trade-off between sophistication and flexibility in individual studies and reusability in centralized databases, and that a decentralized interoperability layer can substantially reduce it. The thesis then develops a framework for the \emph{decentralized} publication of interoperable process classes, generalizing the v0 prototype beyond its original plastics scope. A compatibility layer wraps technology models of any complexity (fixed inventories, machine-learning surrogates, phenomenological models) and (a) enforces tests of physical plausibility, (b) separates parameters set by the base scenario (e.g., grid mix, vintage, efficiency frontier) from those set by the decision (design, operation), so that instances are consistent with macro-scenarios, and (c) exposes the flows needed to connect instances within a p-CLCA system. A central question, long a focus of our group,\ci{Majeau-Bettez2017attribCons,provost-savardSubstitutionModelingCan2024} concerns co-products: when operating conditions change their yields and qualities (e.g., hydrochar from hydrothermal carbonization), the substitutions they induce change too, and the consequential treatment must follow the operating state. We test the infrastructure by packaging models sampled across this corpus, instantiating them for different geographies and vintages, and connecting the instances to one another and to a consequential database. Success is measured by the share of published models wrapped without loss of functionality and passing the plausibility checks, the share of instances that connect consistently, and the effort relative to conventionally inventorying the same variants; we also quantify the effect of disaggregation on consequential scores. \emph{Outcome:} an interoperability framework supporting a decentralized workflow built on quality-checked process classes (design principles and an open interface), with evidence on its fidelity, consistency and effort relative to conventional inventories, from published models and Tasks~2.2--2.3.

\noindent \textbf{Task 2.2. An efficient workflow, from documented to emerging processes} \emph{(O-2; PhD~III, years 2--5).} \emph{Can a human-in-the-loop workflow build functional reduced models from publicly accessible material with much less effort, and with what errors? }%
%
Task~2.1 stakes its design on the expectation that, in the long run, one common, quality-controlled technology model instantiated for different contexts and scenarios will prove more efficient for p-CLCA than inventorying each geographical and vintage variant separately; in the short term, however, developing reduced engineering models remains a major investment. We investigate to what extent data collection, process modelling and coding can be automated with large language model (LLM) agents while keeping the resulting code documented and transparent, in a model-agnostic, human-in-the-loop workflow: agents draft model code from first principles and published material and iterate until the built-in quality controls of Task~2.1 pass, people review it, and every parameter is traced to a cited source. In years 2--3, the workflow is evaluated against reference models (our composting, biomethanization and hydrothermal carbonization models, plus at least one with little public documentation, to bound the advantage of well-documented processes), comparing structure, balance closure, outputs and effort; it also drafts a vintage-resolved steel class from published engineering relations, which Task~2.3 confronts with plant data. In years 3--4, it addresses a major uncertainty of low-maturity processes, the flowsheet itself, by assembling alternative configurations from reusable unit-operation modules and design heuristics,\ci{douglasHierarchicalDecisionProcedure1985} with maturity expressed through physical parameters (yields, specific energy) rather than generic learning rates, after checking that it recovers the configuration of a known process. In years 4--5, it is applied to plasma nitrogen fixation as a potential alternative to both fungicides and Haber-Bosch fertilizers, building on the ongoing PLANET project and collaborations, with configurations evaluated under the electricity and fertilizer-demand contexts of the base scenarios (Tasks~1.1 and~1.3). \emph{Outcome:} an evaluated workflow with its error profile, and a method to represent an undeployed process as a set of plausible configurations.

\noindent \textbf{Task 2.3. Calibrating reduced models of established processes: steel} \emph{(O-2; MSc~II, years 2--3).} \emph{How far do engineering models of an established process deviate from reported plant performance, and does a calibrated, vintage-resolved model change consequential results relative to generic datasets?} %
%
First-principles reduced models (Task~2.2) can quickly grow a base of reusable classes but may miss real-world performance issues; Task~2.3 tests them against well-documented plant data. It takes the steel class drafted in Task~2.2, assembles reference plant and sector data with ArcelorMittal, and adds a calibration step to the workflow, estimating the inefficiencies that design models leave out (part load, start-ups, losses). The calibrated class is instantiated along prospective scenario trajectories (scrap availability from Task~1.2, vintage structure of installed capacity); the efficiency improvements that IAM scenarios assume for the sector are checked against its physical limits; and the class is compared with its uncalibrated version and with ecoinvent datasets, for internal consistency and effect on CLCA results. \emph{Outcome:} a calibrated, reusable steel class, a measure of what generic datasets miss, and a physical consistency check of scenario assumptions for steel.

\section{Integration, risks and broader impact}

Integration does not wait for the end of the program: from year 1, all cases report results in common terms (errors, rank reversals), from which PhD~I and PhD~II build a cross-case synthesis of which simplifications matter, and where. Our cases span energy, metallurgy and fertilizers, which differ in what drives market and process responses, as a framework meant for the many markets of a CLCA requires. Metallurgy and fertilizers are shared by both pillars (centre of the figure). On metallurgy (years 2--3, with ArcelorMittal), Tasks~1.2, 2.2 and~2.3 test whether distinguishing idle, often old, capacity from new, near-best-available capacity changes which supplier responds and with what flows. On fertilizers (years 4--5), Tasks~1.1, 1.3 and~2.2 ask which matters more for an emerging process: uncertainty about its deployed configuration, or about the market responses it triggers (electricity demanded, fertilizer displaced). This integration is interdisciplinary by construction, coupling industrial ecology (LCA, MFA), energy-economic modelling (IAMs, ESMs) and chemical and metallurgical process engineering, with data science as a common means; our HQP are trained at these interfaces.

\noindent \textbf{Risks and fallbacks.} Each task yields publishable results on its own. If perturbing MESSAGE-GLOBIOM proves infeasible, Tasks~1.1 and~1.3 fall back on the open-source IAM GCAM. Should steel data prove inaccessible, Tasks~1.2 (hindcast) and~2.3 switch to aluminium. If the AI-assisted workflow underperforms, its measured error profile is itself a result, and classes are built conventionally. If plasma results are too immature, another process is used.

\noindent \textbf{Open software and its maintenance.} Both frameworks are released as open-source software (versioned releases, continuous integration, documentation) and feed tools the community maintains (Premise, Brightway, Regioinvent, mescal), with models, data and publications released openly. The Pillar~II interface and its quality checks are open, while instances and parameter sets may be published under other licences, provided they pass the same checks, so that contributors can share models without a central gatekeeper. Maintenance of the core is supported by CIRAIG (International Reference Centre for Life Cycle Assessment and Sustainable Transition), whose industrial chairs have been funded by partners for 25 years.

\noindent \textbf{Collaborations and partners.} The program is conducted with partners of CIRAIG's International Research Consortium, notably ArcelorMittal (steel), Hydro-Québec (electricity) and OCP (phosphorus), who contribute data, help define the decision options compared in each case, and serve as early adopters of the resulting CLCA workflows. Joint research\ci{hungECOPT2AdaptableLife2022,pedneaultWhatFuturePrimary2021} and ongoing collaborations with IIASA support the IAM work of Pillar~I, while process engineers and metallurgists of Polytechnique~Montréal's chemical engineering department bring complementary expertise to Pillar~II.

\noindent \textbf{EDI considerations in the research.} EDI bears on both what our models reveal and what they assume. CLCA asks not only how large the consequences of a decision are, but where they fall: which mines, smelters or power plants respond, and therefore which communities bear the burdens.\ci{holland2016electricvehicles,owen2023landconnected} Generic, averaged datasets hide this distribution; our research makes it visible by resolving marginal suppliers to local markets and, where data allow, to plants (Tasks~1.2 and~1.3),\ci{pellanDatasetMetalrelatedProduction2025} and technology variants by age and emission controls, since a minority of old, poorly controlled units concentrates pollution where these fleets operate.\ci{tong2018superpolluting} Results are reported by region and compared with public spatial data relevant in Canada (income, Indigenous lands, remote regions), supporting analyses of distributional justice.\ci{jenkins2016energyjustice} Conversely, our modelling choices determine whose futures the base scenarios represent, whose economic logic drives market responses, whose data inform process models, and whose values set rankings; we make them explicit rather than treating them as technical defaults. (i) Base scenarios embed assumptions about growth and inequality; we include low-demand pathways, which mainstream sets under-represent.\ci{debortoliMoreRationalityInclusivity2025,keysser2021degrowth} (ii) Marginal-supplier methods assume least-cost investment, placing new capacity where production is cheapest, with fairness implications;\ci{pachauri2022fairness} the framework records the assumptions behind each market response. (iii) LCA databases, engineering literature and AI models are skewed towards high-income regions,\ci{manvi2024llmgeobias} so the evaluation of Task~2.2 includes process instances outside high-income countries where reference data exist, and recontextualized instances carry data-quality flags. (iv) Time horizons and weighting sets can reverse rankings, and preferred weights differ between income groups,\ci{deschryver2013valuechoices,bayazitsubasi2024weighting} so rankings are reported under alternative value sets. Each case records these choices in a shared assumptions register reviewed with trainees.

\noindent \textbf{Significance.} The program targets the core limitation that has kept CLCA marginal: its results rest on representations of markets and technologies that are inconsistent with one another and with transition pathways, and whose errors are unknown. Lacking a trusted alternative, decision-makers routinely answer consequential questions with attributional tools, which can mislead them.\ci{Ekvall2004} By deriving market responses and technology representations from the same macro-scale scenarios, this program aims to make p-CLCA consistent and aligned with transition pathways, and to characterize, in contrasting cases, what common simplifications cost. Crucially, it delivers this as open tools that others can adopt and extend, to avoid rebuilding inventories study by study. If successful, its reach would extend beyond our cases, letting the LCA community answer consequential questions with dedicated consequential tools at the resolution of individual design, operation and deployment decisions. For Canadian decision-makers in metallurgy, fertilizer and electricity, it offers a way to test whether their decisions support the transition pathways they plan for.
