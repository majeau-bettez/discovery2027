TRAINING PHILOSOPHY

I believe sustainability and industrial ecology research is particularly challenging for HQP in training. They must integrate the breadth of disciplines needed to capture the many dimensions of a problem while maintaining technical excellence and credibility in each, all within an open and accessible research practice with strong stakeholder engagement and societal implications. I therefore aim to strengthen their critical and pragmatic analytical skills when facing complex questions and systems. Above all, I foster their growing autonomy and independence (encouraging them to take initiatives and risks), mirrored and checked by an equally strong sense of transparency and accountability.

I maintain a group of six to nine HQP under my principal supervision, small enough to truly know each of them. The core of my supervision is a weekly one-on-one meeting with every student. It always starts with how they are doing, and only then turns to their struggles and successes in defining and reaching their next scientific goal. I care that we keep a sense of fun, adventure and discovery… together.

Transparency and openness go both ways: my students know how grants work, the joys of teaching, and when I am, like them, overwhelmed. I give them every opportunity to take part in all dimensions of research and academic life: teaching assistance, scholarship applications, peer review and international conferences.

My HQP and I have the good fortune to work in the truly unique, stimulating and inclusive research environment of the broader CIRAIG team, where students and analysts keep strong ties to both academia and industry. The group is structured to constantly learn from itself. In monthly software lunches, we teach each other the craft of scientific software (version control, testing, code review, documentation), which sustains the culture of reproducible, open modelling at the core of my group. A monthly circular-economy seminar connects our projects to each other and to the broader literature. Students write through shared drafts and workshops, and rehearse talks together. Twice a year, they present at CIRAIG's symposia and to its industrial partners, and discover that someone in the real world cares about their work and will hold it to account.

Starting with this program, each student and I will also revisit a development plan every year for their skills, publications and career.

Several systemic barriers must be addressed. In 2024, women made up only 29% of graduate enrolment in Canadian engineering programs (Engineers Canada, 2026). Our field adds another hurdle: most sustainability literature and tools reflect a European and North American perspective on development. Even in an inclusive and well-meaning group, there is a risk of losing a diversity of student perspectives through a desire to conform to what can be perceived as a universal norm.

In 2024, Polytechnique's EDI office consulted CIRAIG members (Labos inclusifs project). Its report recognized a diverse and caring environment, but also documented barriers I take seriously: students who speak neither French nor English as a first language struggle in a francophone institution; hybrid work led some students to drift into isolation; paid opportunities were not equally visible to all; international students face financial precarity and limited scholarship eligibility; and our team, largely made of engineers, lacks disciplinary diversity.

This report helped me reflect on unintended harms in my own practice. Offering a paid opportunity directly to one student, for example, may prove inequitable; I have shifted to open calls and group discussions of opportunities. My own Western perspective also inevitably shapes which questions I notice. I therefore make a conscious, active effort to recognize my limits when student initiatives challenge my assumptions, supported by a written reflective diary and Polytechnique's "Pour un campus inclusif" training.

Together with CIRAIG, our response is concrete. We recruit through open postings shared beyond my own networks, screen anonymized CVs first, include a colleague of another gender on every selection and hiring committee, and welcome non-engineering and non-linear backgrounds. Newcomers are paired with a peer, and we make room for language exchanges and courses throughout HQP training. Students work daily alongside female scientific leaders: CIRAIG is led by a woman, and half of its professors are women. Its new permanent EDI committee brings in external trainers and has set up a person-protection protocol with clearly displayed resources. All students receive the same funding, above Polytechnique's recommended level, whatever their scholarship eligibility. They organize their own schedules in a spirit of autonomy and mutual trust, while shared in-person days keep us connected. A yearly retreat in nature brings us together, and I will keep insisting that everyone take at least four weeks of real vacation. Each year, an analyst with no authority over the students holds confidential interviews so that problems are voiced unfiltered. Finally, students will learn to document in a shared register whose scenarios, data and values their models carry, and to review these choices with me.

RESEARCH TRAINING PLAN

Formal training is lacking in the core tools of our research (dynamic MFA, consequential LCA, input-output and macro-scale models). I am therefore developing a graduate course, Industrial Ecology Modelling, offered at Polytechnique Montréal from 2027, to give all HQP in this program a common foundation.

PhD I (Task 1.1, years 1–4), co-supervised by Edgar Hertwich (IIASA), will learn to perturb integrated assessment and energy system models (MESSAGE-GLOBIOM, EnergyScope-Quebec) and to translate their responses into market mechanisms for CLCA. Graduate courses in operations research and optimization will lay the foundation, and a summer at IIASA (Young Scientists Summer Program) will immerse the student among these models' developers. As MSc I and III will use this software, PhD I will also learn to write tools for others.

PhD II (Task 2.1, years 1–4) will design the interoperability framework for process models. We aim to recruit a student with a software engineering background, whose training will therefore focus on the complementary skills of process design and chemical engineering, through graduate courses in our chemical engineering department and co-supervision by François Maréchal (EPFL, CIRAIG), an expert in process and systems engineering.

Conversely, PhD III (Task 2.2, years 2–5) will complement a strong engineering background with courses in machine learning, AI-assisted programming and Bayesian statistics, needed to partially automate, through iterative improvement cycles, the development of reduced models across many technologies. Above all, the student will learn to judge critically what AI agents produce. For the exploratory modelling of emerging technologies, the student will be co-supervised by Jason Tavares, a specialist in process design and agricultural process innovation (PLANET project).

MSc I (Task 1.2, years 2–3), co-supervised by Manuele Margni (HES-SO, CIRAIG), will learn, with ArcelorMittal, dynamic material flow analysis and the detective work of testing models against what industry actually built; Margni will help keep this data work tied to method questions.

MSc II (Task 2.3, years 2–3), co-supervised by Jean-Philippe Harvey, an expert in thermodynamics and metallurgy, will learn to calibrate a process model on plant data and to test scenario assumptions through thermodynamic reasoning, ideally during a Mitacs internship at ArcelorMittal.

MSc III (Task 1.3, years 4–5) will learn spatial disaggregation and distributional reporting on fertilizer markets, with OCP and the GLOBIOM team. Recruiting from geography or data science will deliberately widen our group's disciplines.

Undergraduate summer interns will compile plant capacities and published process models (Tasks 1.2 and 2.1), and get a first taste of reproducible, code-based research.

What I value most is that students will not work alone. They will share one codebase and review each other's code, and PhD students will learn to mentor the MSc students who build on their tools. On steel (years 2–3) and fertilizers (years 4–5), students from both pillars will learn to reconcile market and process perspectives, while PhD I and II lead the synthesis across cases. Working with industrial partners (ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec, OCP), students will learn to turn a partner's question into a research question. Each PhD should publish three first-author articles and present at two international conferences; each MSc, one article. Above all, I hope they will leave as independent, critical and generous agents of change for sustainability, in Canada and abroad.
