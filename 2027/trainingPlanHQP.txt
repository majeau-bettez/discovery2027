TRAINING PHILOSOPHY

Research is an adventure, and I want my students to feel its excitement: the pleasure of a question that turns out to be deeper than expected, of a model that finally closes its balances, of a result that surprises us. I want them to feel, just as strongly, a responsibility to produce knowledge that helps people make better decisions for a sustainable future. Holding both together is what I try to teach. In practice, this means accompanying each student from guided work toward genuine autonomy, with autonomy growing hand in hand with accountability for their results, their code and their claims. I maintain a group of six to nine HQP under my principal supervision, small enough that I truly know each of them.

The core of my supervision is a weekly one-on-one meeting with every student. It always starts with how they are doing, and only then turns to the science, the next steps and, over time, their career. Around these meetings, the group learns from itself. In Midi-outils, students teach each other the craft of scientific software: version control, testing, code review, documentation. Open, reproducible research is not an add-on in my group; it is how we work. A monthly circular-economy seminar keeps us talking with other disciplines. Students write through shared drafts and workshops, rehearse talks together, and take part in proposals, peer reviews and teaching. Twice a year, they present at CIRAIG's symposia and to its industrial partners, and discover that someone in the real world cares about their work and will hold it to account. Starting with this program, every student and I will revisit a development plan each year for their skills, publications and career, and I will continue to support their scholarship applications and first teaching and supervision experiences. I also let them peek behind the curtain: they know how grants work, what pays their scholarship, and when I am overwhelmed. I would rather discuss the constraints of research careers openly than let students discover them alone.

Barriers we must address. Women made up 27% of Canadian undergraduate engineering enrolment in 2024, and Indigenous students 0.7%. Our own field adds a geographic bias: LCA knowledge, databases and methods are produced mainly in Europe and North America. In 2024, Polytechnique's EDI office consulted CIRAIG members (Labos inclusifs project). Its report recognized a diverse and caring environment, but also documented barriers I take seriously: students who speak neither French nor English as a first language struggle in a francophone institution that offers no French courses, while 46% of its graduate students come from abroad; hybrid work can quietly isolate people; paid opportunities were not equally visible to all; international students face financial precarity and limited scholarship eligibility; and our team is mostly made of engineers trained in Europe or North America.

Reading this report was a lesson in self-reflection. Habits I thought harmless, such as offering a paid opportunity directly to a student I knew was available, felt inequitable to others. And my own European-trained engineering perspective inevitably shapes whom I recruit and which questions I notice. I now treat these as practices to correct rather than intentions to defend.

Our response is concrete. We recruit through open postings shared beyond my own networks, screen anonymized CVs first, include a colleague of another gender on every committee, and welcome non-engineering and non-linear backgrounds. All opportunities (paid work, conferences, teaching) are now posted to the whole group. Newcomers are paired with a peer, and either language is welcome in our meetings. CIRAIG has created a permanent EDI committee, which brings in external trainers and has set up a person-protection protocol with clearly displayed resources. All students receive the same funding, above Polytechnique's recommended level, whatever their scholarship eligibility. They organize their own schedules, and I trust them to; a shared in-person day will keep us connected, a yearly retreat in nature will bring us together, and I will keep insisting that everyone take at least four weeks of real vacation. Each year, an analyst with no authority over the students holds confidential interviews so that problems reach me unfiltered. Finally, in this program students will learn to document, in a shared register, whose scenarios, data and values their models carry, and we will review these choices together.

RESEARCH TRAINING PLAN

This program will train three PhD and three MSc students, joined by one or two undergraduate interns. Each student will own one task of the proposal, and will be co-supervised, where relevant, by a colleague chosen for the expertise that task demands. All will take my new graduate course, Industrial Ecology Modelling, which I am developing to give them a common language across material flow analysis, consequential LCA, input-output and macro-scale models.

PhD I (Task 1.1, years 1–4), co-supervised by Edgar Hertwich at IIASA, will learn to perturb integrated assessment and energy system models (MESSAGE-GLOBIOM, EnergyScope-Quebec), to run them at scale on Calcul Québec, and to translate their responses into market mechanisms for CLCA. Graduate courses in operations research and optimization will lay the foundation, and a summer at IIASA (Young Scientists Summer Program) will immerse the student among the people who build these models. Because MSc I and MSc III will later use the student's software, PhD I will also learn to write tools for others.

PhD II (Task 2.1, years 1–4) will train as a research software engineer for LCA. A software-minded engineer or computer scientist, the student will learn software architecture, data models and ontologies [course to confirm], testing and continuous integration, through intensive Brightway doctoral training and close work with the CFI 2PURES developers and the Brightway and Premise communities. As PhD III and MSc II will build on the student's interface, PhD II will learn early to design for other users.

PhD III (Task 2.2, years 2–5), co-supervised by Jason Tavares, a specialist in process design and plasma, will bring a process engineering background and add Bayesian statistics, machine learning and AI-assisted modelling. The heart of this training will be judgement: the student will learn to use AI agents critically, to check whether a drafted model is physically sound, to trace every parameter to its source, and to recognize when engineering knowledge must override a fluent but wrong answer. Hands-on work on flowsheet synthesis and on plasma nitrogen fixation, within the PLANET project, will anchor these skills in real processes.

MSc I (Task 1.2, years 2–3), co-supervised by Manuele Margni (HES-SO and CIRAIG), will learn dynamic material flow analysis and the detective work of reconstructing past investments from plant and company data, with ArcelorMittal, and will learn from Margni to keep data work tied to method questions.

MSc II (Task 2.3, years 2–3), co-supervised by Jean-Philippe Harvey, an expert in thermodynamics and metallurgy, will learn to calibrate a process model on plant data and to use thermodynamic reasoning to test the assumptions of scenarios, ideally through a Mitacs internship at ArcelorMittal.

MSc III (Task 1.3, years 4–5) will learn spatial disaggregation and distributional reporting on fertilizer markets with OCP and the GLOBIOM team. We will recruit from geography or data science, deliberately widening the disciplines of our group.

Undergraduate summer interns will compile plant capacities and published process models (Tasks 1.2 and 2.1), and get a first taste of reproducible, code-based research.

What I value most in this program is that students will not work alone. They will share one codebase and review each other's code. MSc students will start from tools the PhD students built, and PhD students will learn to mentor by supporting them. On steel (years 2–3) and on fertilizers (years 4–5), students from both pillars will work on the same cases and learn to reconcile market and process perspectives, and PhD I and II will learn to lead a synthesis across disciplines. Each student will work with an industrial partner (ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec, OCP) and learn to turn a partner's question into a research question. Each PhD should publish three first-author articles and present at two international conferences; each MSc, one article. Graduates who combine macro-scale modelling, process engineering and open software are rare, and sought after across sectors. Above all, I hope they will leave as independent, critical and generous researchers.
