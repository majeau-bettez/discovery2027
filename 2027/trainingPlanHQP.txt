TRAINING PHILOSOPHY

I aim to train researchers who build models others can trust: rigorous, open about their assumptions, and tied to the decisions they inform. Trainees move from guided work to autonomy, with autonomy matched by growing accountability for their results and code. I maintain a group of 6–9 HQP under my principal supervision.

Mentoring. Each HQP has a weekly one-on-one meeting on science, progress and career, which starts with how they are doing. I lead two discussion groups: Midi-outils, on scientific software practice (version control, testing, code review, documentation), and a monthly circular-economy seminar open to other disciplines. HQP write through shared drafts and writing workshops, rehearse talks before conferences, and contribute to proposals, peer reviews and teaching material. Twice a year they present at CIRAIG's internal symposia and to its industrial partners, learning to speak to the people who will use their results. I am open about research life (funding, workload, setbacks) so that its constraints can be discussed rather than endured. Each HQP sets a yearly development plan with me (skills, publications, career goals) and is supported in scholarship applications, teaching assistantships and the co-supervision of undergraduate projects.

Barriers. Women made up 27% of Canadian undergraduate engineering enrolment in 2024, and Indigenous students 0.7%; LCA knowledge, its databases and its methods are produced mainly in Europe and North America. A 2024 consultation of CIRAIG members by Polytechnique's EDI office (Labos inclusifs) documented local barriers: language for students who speak neither French nor English as a first language, in a francophone institution with no French courses and 46% international graduate students; isolation in hybrid work; unequal access to paid opportunities; financial precarity of international students with limited scholarship eligibility; and a team mostly of engineers trained in Europe or North America.

Self-reflection. The consultation showed me that informal habits I thought harmless, such as offering paid opportunities directly to some students, felt inequitable to others, and that my European-trained engineering perspective shapes whom I recruit and which questions I see. I now treat these as practices to correct, not intentions to defend.

Practices. (i) Recruitment: open postings shared beyond my networks, anonymized CVs at first screening, a committee including a colleague of another gender, and openness to non-engineering and non-linear backgrounds. (ii) Inclusive culture: all opportunities (paid work, conferences, teaching) posted to the whole group; peer pairing for newcomers; meetings where either language is welcome; CIRAIG's new permanent EDI committee, which provides external EDI training and a person-protection protocol with visible resources. (iii) Equity of means: all HQP receive equal funding above Polytechnique's recommended level, regardless of scholarship eligibility; flexible schedules, a shared in-person day against isolation, and at least four weeks of vacation. (iv) Accountability: each year, an analyst with no authority over trainees holds confidential interviews; findings are discussed with the group. (v) Social context: each case keeps an assumptions register (whose scenarios, data and values the models carry), reviewed with trainees, which links their training to the EDI considerations of the research itself.

RESEARCH TRAINING PLAN

The program trains three PhD and three MSc students, plus one or two undergraduates, each owning one task. Co-supervisors bring the expertise each task needs. All HQP take my new graduate course, Industrial Ecology Modelling (MFA, consequential LCA, input-output and macro-models), designed for this skill set.

PhD I (Task 1.1, years 1–4; co-supervised by Edgar Hertwich, IIASA). Energy or industrial engineering profile. Learns to perturb IAMs (MESSAGE-GLOBIOM) and ESMs (EnergyScope-Quebec), to run them on Calcul Québec, and to model market responses for CLCA; graduate courses in operations research and optimization; a summer at IIASA (Young Scientists Summer Program). Hertwich brings IAM and industrial ecology expertise and access to IIASA's modelling teams. First deliverable (year 2): the perturbation protocol and first EnergyScope-Quebec responses. Builds the framework used by MSc I and MSc III.

PhD II (Task 2.1, years 1–4). Software-oriented engineering or computer science profile. Learns software architecture, data models and ontologies, testing and continuous integration, and consequential co-product modelling; graduate course on [ontologies/knowledge representation]; intensive Brightway doctoral training; works with the CFI 2PURES developers and the Brightway and Premise communities. First deliverable (year 2): the diagnostic of process parametrization in consequential ecoinvent. Defines the interface that PhD III and MSc II populate, and so learns to design for other users.

PhD III (Task 2.2, years 2–5; co-supervised by Jason Tavares, chemical engineering). Process engineering profile. Adds Bayesian statistics and machine learning (graduate courses), LLM-agent workflows under human review, flowsheet synthesis, and plasma processes within the PLANET project. Tavares brings process design and plasma expertise. The task requires judging when an automatically drafted model is physically sound, so engineering judgement remains the core skill; AI is a means, used with traceable sources. Drafts the steel class for MSc II.

MSc I (Task 1.2, years 2–3; co-supervised by Manuele Margni, HES-SO and CIRAIG). Learns dynamic MFA, plant and company data, and retrospective testing of CLCA methods, with ArcelorMittal. Margni brings consequential LCA methodology, so that data work stays tied to method questions.

MSc II (Task 2.3, years 2–3; co-supervised by Jean-Philippe Harvey, chemical engineering). Learns metallurgical thermodynamics, calibration on plant data and vintage-resolved models; Mitacs internship at ArcelorMittal. Harvey brings thermodynamics and metallurgy, needed to check the efficiency gains that scenarios assume against physical limits.

MSc III (Task 1.3, years 4–5). Learns spatial disaggregation, fertilizer markets and distributional reporting, with OCP and the GLOBIOM team; geography or data science profile, which widens the group's disciplines.

Undergraduates (USRA). Compile plant capacities (Task 1.2) and published process models (Task 2.1), with a first exposure to reproducible code.

Across the program. HQP share one codebase with mutual code reviews; MSc students start from the PhDs' tools, and PhDs learn to mentor by supporting them. Two cohorts mix market and process perspectives on common cases: steel (years 2–3) and fertilizers (years 4–5). From year 1, PhD I and II lead the cross-case synthesis, a training in integrating research across disciplines. Each HQP works with an industrial partner (ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec, OCP) who helps define the decision options, and learns to turn partner questions into research questions. Each PhD is expected to publish three first-author articles and present at two international conferences, each MSc one article. These profiles, combining macro-scale modelling, process engineering and open software, are sought by research institutes, government modelling units, consulting firms and industrial sustainability teams, as well as academia.
