TRAINING PHILOSOPHY

I believe sustainability and industrial ecology research is particularly challenging for HQPs in training. There is a tension between integrating the breadth of disciplines necessary to capture the different dimensions of a problem and maintaining a high level of technical excellence and credibility in each, all that in an open and accessible research practice with strong stakeholder engagement and societal implications. I therefore aim to strengthen in my students their critical and pragmatic analytical skills when confronted with complex questions and systems. I strive above all to foster a growing autonomy and independence in their research (encouraging them to take initiatives and risks), mirrored and checked by an equally important sense of transparency and accountability.


I maintain a group of six to nine HQP under my principal supervision, small enough that I truly get to know each of them. The core of my supervision is a weekly one-on-one meeting with every student. It always starts with how they are doing, and only then turns to their  struggles and successes in achieving or defining their next scientific goal. It is important to me that we maintain a sense of fun, adventure and discovery… together.

Transparency and openness goes both ways: my students know how grants works, the joys of teaching, and when I am overwhelmed just like them. In fact, I try to give them maximum opportunity to involve themselves in every dimension of research and academic life (teaching assistance, scholarship applications, peer-review, international conferences, etc.)

My HQPs and I have the chance of evolving in the truly unique, stimulating and inclusive research environment of the broader CIRAIG team, bringing together students and analysts who maintain a strong connection both to academia and industry. We have structured this environment such that the group is constantly learning from itself. In monthly software-lunches, we teach each other the craft of scientific software: version control, testing, code review, documentation, novel approaches. This supports a culture of modelling reproducibility and openness that is core to my group. A monthly circular-economy seminar helps us share across projects and connect to broader literature. Students write through shared drafts and workshops, rehearse talks together, and take part in proposals, peer reviews and teaching. Twice a year, they present at CIRAIG's symposia and to its industrial partners, and discover that someone in the real world cares about their work and will hold it to account.

Starting with this program, every student and I will revisit a development plan each year for their skills, publications and career, and I will continue to support their scholarship applications and first teaching and supervision experiences.

There are systemic barriers we must address. Notably, women made up 27% of Canadian undergraduate engineering enrolment in 2024, and Indigenous students 0.7%.

 In 2024, Polytechnique's EDI office consulted CIRAIG members (Labos inclusifs project). Its report recognized a diverse and caring environment, but also documented barriers I take seriously: students who speak neither French nor English as a first language struggle in a francophone institution, while 46% of its graduate students come from abroad; hybrid work can quietly isolate people; paid opportunities were not equally visible to all; international students face financial precarity and limited scholarship eligibility; and our team is mostly made of engineers trained in Europe or North America.

This report helped me reflect on potential unintended harms in my own practice. For exemple, directly offering a paid opportunity directly to a student may prove inequitable; and I shifted to more formal open offerings and convesations around opportunities. Also, my own Western perspective inevitably shapes which questions I notice, hence a conscious effort to be particularly mindful of my own limitations when student initiatives confront my assumptions.

Our response is concrete. We recruit through open postings shared beyond my own networks, screen anonymized CVs first, include a colleague of another gender on every committee, and welcome non-engineering and non-linear backgrounds. Newcomers are paired with a peer, and  we make room for language exchange events and courses throughout HQP training. CIRAIG has created a permanent EDI committee, which brings in external trainers and has set up a person-protection protocol with clearly displayed resources. All students receive the same funding, above Polytechnique's recommended level, whatever their scholarship eligibility. They organize their own schedules in a spirit of autonomy and mutual trust; a shared in-person day keeps us connected. A yearly retreat in nature brings us together, and I will keep insisting that everyone take at least four weeks of real vacation. Each year, an analyst with no authority over the students holds confidential interviews so that problems are voiced unfiltered. Finally, in this program students will learn to document, in a shared register, whose scenarios, data and values their models carry, and we will review these choices together.

RESEARCH TRAINING PLAN


To address the lack of formal training on the techniques and tools of our research (dynamic MFA, consequential LCA, input-output and macro-scale models, etc.), I am in the process of developing a new course on Industrial Ecology Modelling, which will be offered starting next year at Polytechnique Montreal. This will prove relevant for a common foundation for all HQPs in this program.

PhD I (Task 1.1, years 1–4), co-supervised by Edgar Hertwich at IIASA, will learn to perturb integrated assessment and energy system models (MESSAGE-GLOBIOM, EnergyScope-Quebec), and to translate their responses into market mechanisms for CLCA. Graduate courses in operations research and optimization will lay the foundation, and a summer at IIASA (Young Scientists Summer Program) will immerse the student among the people who build these models. Because MSc I and MSc III will later use the student's software, PhD I will also learn to write tools for others.

To design a process-model interoperability framework (Task 2.1, years 1–4), the ideal HQP profile for PhD III would be a student with a software engineering background, and the training plan would therefore focus on complementary skills in process design and chemical engineering. This will be facilitated by the many process-focused courses and potential cosupervisions within our Chemical engineering department.

The training of PhD III (Task 2.2, years 2–5) will likely need to complement a strong engineering background with courses on machine learning, AI-assisted modelling and Bayesian statistics. These will prove essential in partially automating the development of reduced models across a broad range of technologies.  The heart of this training will be judgement: the student will learn to use AI agents critically, to check whether a drafted model is physically sound, to trace every parameter to its source, and to recognize when ground data is insufficient. In the more explorative modelling of emerging technologies, this HQP will benefit from the cosupervision of Jason Tavares, a specialist in process design and innovation in agricultural processes, notably in the PLANET project.

MSc I (Task 1.2, years 2–3), co-supervised by Manuele Margni (HES-SO and CIRAIG), will learn dynamic material flow analysis and the detective work of reconstructing past investments from plant and company data, with ArcelorMittal, and will learn from Margni to keep data work tied to method questions.

MSc II (Task 2.3, years 2–3), co-supervised by Jean-Philippe Harvey, an expert in thermodynamics and metallurgy, will learn to calibrate a process model on plant data and to use thermodynamic reasoning to test the assumptions of scenarios, ideally through a Mitacs internship at ArcelorMittal.

MSc III (Task 1.3, years 4–5) will learn spatial disaggregation and distributional reporting on fertilizer markets with OCP and the GLOBIOM team. We will recruit from geography or data science, deliberately widening the disciplines of our group.

Undergraduate summer interns will compile plant capacities and published process models (Tasks 1.2 and 2.1), and get a first taste of reproducible, code-based research.

What I value most in this program is that students will not work alone. They will share one codebase and review each other's code. MSc students will start from tools the PhD students built, and PhD students will learn to mentor by supporting them. On steel (years 2–3) and on fertilizers (years 4–5), students from both pillars will work on the same cases and learn to reconcile market and process perspectives, and PhD I and II will learn to lead a synthesis across disciplines. Each student will work with an industrial partner (ArcelorMittal, Hydro-Québec, OCP) and learn to turn a partner's question into a research question. Each PhD should publish three first-author articles and present at two international conferences; each MSc, one article. Graduates who combine macro-scale modelling, process engineering and open software are rare, and sought after across sectors. Above all, I hope they will leave as independent, critical and generous agents of change.
