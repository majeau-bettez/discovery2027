Firms, governments and consumers increasingly choose products, technologies and investments based on their environmental footprint, calculated by life cycle assessment (LCA). Most footprints answer an accounting question: what share of today's emissions belongs to this product? Decisions raise a different question: what will change in the world if we choose this option? During a transition, the answers can diverge sharply. A plant already running on clean hydroelectricity sees little benefit in saving electricity, yet every kilowatt-hour it saves could replace fossil fuels elsewhere. A product made with more recycled aluminium looks better, yet if scrap is scarce, it may simply divert recycled metal from another product, which then uses new metal.

A branch of LCA was created two decades ago to answer the second question, by tracing how markets and technologies respond to a decision. It has remained marginal. For thousands of products, it must anticipate which producers would respond to a change in demand, and how their plants would operate. Unable to model this in detail, analysts rely on rules of thumb, each study its own, usually assuming the future will resemble the present. The results are inconsistent, costly, and hard to trust, as no one knows how wrong the shortcuts are.

Our program answers these weaknesses with one strategy. First, against inconsistency, we anchor every assessment in the same pictures of the future that already guide climate and energy policy: scenarios of global and national energy-economy models. Each decision is analysed as a small push on this shared future. Second, against unknown errors, since most products cannot be modelled in detail, we measure what each shortcut costs, testing it against detailed models, past investments and real plant data, and asking: does it change which option comes out best? Third, against cost, we turn this work into open software, so that a tested model of a market or a technology, such as a steel plant, can be reused and adapted to new places and future years, rather than rebuilt each time.

We apply this strategy to two questions: which producers respond to a decision, and how they produce, depending on age, location, design and operation. Cases cover electricity in Quebec and worldwide, steel and aluminium, fertilizers, and an emerging plasma process to fix nitrogen, with partners such as Hydro-Québec, ArcelorMittal and OCP. We also test how AI can speed up building transparent, verified process models.

For LCA practice, this is a deep change: environmental data would no longer be a catalogue of fixed recipes, but a shared library of models that respond to the future we expect and to the decision at hand. For Canadian industry and governments, it offers a way to check that individual decisions support the transition they plan for. Six graduate students will train at the junction of environmental assessment, energy-economic modelling and process engineering.
