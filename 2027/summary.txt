Companies, governments and consumers increasingly choose between products, technologies and investments based on their environmental footprint, calculated by life cycle assessment (LCA). Most footprints answer an accounting question: what share of today's emissions belongs to this product? Decisions raise a different question: what will change in the world if we choose this option? In an economy in rapid transition, the two answers can diverge sharply. A plant already running on clean hydroelectricity sees little benefit in saving electricity, yet every kilowatt-hour it saves could replace fossil fuels elsewhere. A product made with more recycled aluminium looks better, yet if scrap is scarce, it may simply divert recycled metal from another product, which then uses new metal instead.

A branch of LCA was created two decades ago to answer the second question, by tracing how markets and technologies respond to a decision. It has remained marginal. For each of thousands of products, it must anticipate which producers would respond to a change in demand, and how their plants would operate. Unable to model all this in detail, analysts rely on rules of thumb, each study with its own, usually assuming that the future will look like the present. The results are inconsistent, costly, and hard to trust, because no one knows how wrong the shortcuts are.

Our program answers these weaknesses with one strategy. First, against inconsistency, we anchor every assessment in the same pictures of the future that already guide climate and energy policy: the scenarios of global and national energy-economy models. Each decision is analysed as a small push on this shared future. Second, against unknown errors, we accept that most products cannot be modelled in detail, but we measure what each shortcut costs: we test simplified rules against detailed models, against the plants that were actually built, and against real plant data, and we ask a practical question: does the shortcut change which option comes out best? Third, against cost, we turn this work into open, shared software, so that a tested model of a market or a technology, such as a steel plant, can be reused and adapted by others to new places and future years, rather than rebuilt in every study.

We apply this strategy to two questions: which producers respond to a decision, and how they produce, depending on their age, location, design and operation. We test it on electricity in Quebec and worldwide, steel and aluminium, nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers, and an emerging technology that fixes nitrogen with electric plasma, with partners including Hydro-Québec, ArcelorMittal and OCP. We also test how artificial intelligence can speed up the building of transparent, verified models of industrial processes.

For LCA practice, this is a deep change: environmental data would no longer be a catalogue of fixed recipes, but a shared library of models that respond to the future we expect and to the decision being made. For Canadian industry and governments, it offers a way to check that individual decisions support the transition they plan for. The program will train six graduate students at the junction of environmental assessment, energy-economic modelling and process engineering.
